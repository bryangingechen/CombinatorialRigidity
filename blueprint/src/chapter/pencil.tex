\section{Pencil realizations: hinges concurrent within a common panel}
\label{sec:pencil}

In dimension three, this chapter and \cref{sec:main-component}
strengthen KT Theorem~5.6 in the hinge-coplanar model of
\cref{sec:molecular-coplanar-multigraph}. A pencil realization gives
each body, besides its panel, a point of that panel, through which every
hinge at the body passes (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}). The
hinges at a body then form a pencil of lines through a point in a plane.
We show that, over every infinite field, every spanning multigraph $G$
has a pencil realization of rank
$6(|V(G)| - 1) - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$, the deficiency rank of
\cref{thm:theorem-55-6-multigraph}. If $G$ is simple, the realization
can be chosen with the points of adjacent bodies projectively distinct.
If moreover $G$ has a nondegenerate pencil realization
(\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}), it has one of that rank. These claims
make up \cref{thm:pencil-realization-theorem}. Besides
\cref{sec:molecular-coplanar-multigraph}, the reader needs the
deficiency and rigid subgraphs of \cref{sec:molecular-deficiency}.

\Cref{sec:pencil-roadmap} gives a road map of the proof, across this
chapter, \cref{sec:main-component} and \cref{sec:pencil-d}.
\Cref{sec:pencil-through-point} and \cref{sec:pencil-realization} give
the definitions. \Cref{sec:pencil-extension} gives an added edge a
hinge through the points of both its ends. Such a hinge exists only when
the point of each end lies in the panel of the other
(\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}). These conditions can fail at the
edges that Katoh and Tanigawa add to a realization of a minimal spanning
subgraph. So we induct over every multigraph, not only over the minimal
ones (\cref{thm:pencil-reduction}). \Cref{sec:pencil-reduction}
states this induction, whose cases are proved in every dimension
(\cref{sec:pencil-d-reduction}). \Cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate} adds the
distinct points and the nondegenerate realizations to the statement, the
conditioned pair. Katoh and Tanigawa's induction statement likewise asks
for nonparallel realizations (KT Theorem~5.5).
\Cref{sec:pencil-main-component-route} sketches how
\cref{sec:main-component} builds pencil realizations by lifting pictures
of $G$ in the plane, as in scene analysis, and proves the theorem. The
road map names the classical sources of this lifting, and the parts of
the proof that are new.

Two parts of the chapter are not used in the proof of
\cref{thm:pencil-realization-theorem}. \Cref{sec:pencil-duality} shows that the
polarity of \cref{lem:polarity-join} takes a pencil
realization to one with the panel normals and the points exchanged.
This chapter uses the polarity nowhere else. \Cref{sec:pencil-cycle}
realizes the cycles on three and four bodies as pencil realizations.

\subsection{Road map of the proof}
\label{sec:pencil-roadmap}

We prove \cref{thm:pencil-realization-theorem} as the case $d = 3$ of a
theorem in every dimension $d \ge 3$
(\cref{cor:pencil-d-conditioned-pair}). Over an infinite field, every
multigraph with at least one body has a pencil realization at the
deficiency rank in dimension $d$. If it is simple, it has an
adjacent-distinct one, which at $d = 3$ means that the points of
adjacent bodies are distinct. If it is moreover nondegeneracy-feasible,
that is, if it has a nondegenerate realization at all, then it has a
nondegenerate one at the deficiency rank.

This subsection describes the proof as a whole, in the manner of Katoh
and Tanigawa's road map of their own proof (KT~\S1.1,
pp.~649--650)~\cite{katohTanigawa2011}. They run one combinatorial
induction over the minimal $k$-dof-graphs, with three cases (KT~\S4).
They then implement each case geometrically (KT~\S6). Our proof has
three parts, each stated and proved in every dimension $d \ge 3$.
Part~A, the reduction, is an induction over all multigraphs, and gives
the first two claims (\cref{thm:pencil-d-realization-theorem}). Part~B,
the main-component induction, shows that a general configuration lifted
from a picture in the plane has the deficiency rank
(\cref{thm:pencil-d-coverage}). Part~C, the generic conjunct, is an
induction over the lifts of nondegenerate realizations in dimension
three, and gives the third claim (\cref{thm:pencil-d-generic}). Both A
and C use B.

The setting of Part~B is classical. A picture of $G$ in the plane, with
heights at which the points of every closed neighbourhood are coplanar,
is a scene over a plane picture of an incidence structure, in the sense
of scene analysis~\cite[\S\S 2.1--2.2]{whiteley1984},
\cite[\S 2.2]{sugihara1984}. The points of the structure are the bodies
of $G$, and its faces are the closed neighbourhoods
(\cref{sec:main-component-carrier}). The lifting space $L(q)$ of a
picture $q$ is the space of the heights of these scenes. Lifting a plane
figure to a polyhedron goes back to Maxwell: a plane figure has a
reciprocal figure when it is the projection of a polyhedron with plane
faces~\cite[p.~258]{maxwell1864reciprocal},
\cite[pp.~7--8]{maxwell1870}. Whiteley's correspondence identifies the
scenes over a picture with the motions of a framework of plates and bars
in the plane~\cite[Proposition~4.1]{whiteley1984}. For our incidence
structure this framework is pin-collinear in the sense of Jackson and
Jord\'an~\cite[p.~9]{jacksonJordan2006pin}
(\cref{lem:pencil-lifting-planes-motions}).

The correspondence grew out
of Whiteley's kinematic proof of Maxwell's theorem on projected
polyhedra~\cite{whiteley1982}, which uses the projective theory of Crapo
and Whiteley~\cite{crapoWhiteley1982}. That proof concerns oriented
polyhedra. It begins by observing that lifting a plane picture to a
polyhedral scene is an infinitesimal motion of the panel structure on
the flat projection (p.~14). For any incidence structure, the scenes
over a picture are likewise the motions, normal to the plane, of plates
lying in it and joined at points~\cite[\S\S 5.1, 5.3]{whiteley1984}. So
the motions of a flat configuration normal to its plane are the scenes
over its picture (\cref{lem:pencil-flat-split}).

At a general picture,
Whiteley's picture matroid gives the dimension of the space of scenes
when the incidences are independent~\cite[Theorem~3.1, p.~84]{whiteley1989}. In
general it follows, over the reals, from the rank theorem of Jackson and
Jord\'an~\cite[Theorem~7.1]{jacksonJordan2006pin}
(\cref{sec:main-component-jj}).

Crapo and Ryan lift a picture by all of
its locally linear functions at
once~\cite[\S 8, pp.~52--53]{crapoRyan1986}. With the closed
neighbourhoods as their nodes these functions are the heights in $L(q)$,
and a configuration in dimension $d$ is a linear image of their lift
(\cref{sec:pencil-d-carrier}). The lifts of Part~C are scenes in
$4$-space over points of $3$-space, in Whiteley's extension of scene
analysis to higher dimensions~\cite[Definition~7.2]{whiteley1984}
(\cref{sec:pencil-d-lift}). The name pencil is taken from Crapo and
Whiteley's flat pencil, the lines through a point in a
plane~\cite[\S3.5, p.~67]{crapoWhiteley1982}. The polarity that
exchanges coplanar and concurrent hinges is classical, and Tay and
Whiteley note it for hinge structures~\cite[p.~33]{tayWhiteley1984}.

As far as we have found, the following are new. No source that we have
read asks that the hinges at a body be both coplanar and concurrent, so
the class of pencil realizations itself is new. So are the pencil
realization theorem, the reduction of Part~A, the steps and the coverage of Part~B, and the
induction of Part~C. So is the reading of a picture lifted by several
heights as a hinge framework with a rank. The self-duality of the class
(\cref{lem:pencil-self-dual}) we have not found stated. The sources
above work over the real numbers. The statements of this proof hold
over every infinite field.

\emph{The reduction.} Katoh and Tanigawa realize a multigraph that is
not minimal by realizing a minimal spanning subgraph with the same
deficiency, and adding the remaining edges. For pencil realizations an
added edge asks two cross-incidences of the realization of the smaller
graph (\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}). At $K_4$ and a $4$-cycle in
it, no pencil realization of the $4$-cycle at the deficiency rank meets
them (\cref{sec:pencil-reduction}). So A is an induction over every
multigraph, on $(|V(G)|, |E(G)|)$, with five structural cases
(\cref{thm:pencil-reduction}). The cases of a loop, of at most two
bodies and of a cut edge place hinges in two pencils
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-two-pencil-extension}). At a loopless
two-edge-connected multigraph with parallel edges, two parallel edges
are contracted, as Katoh and Tanigawa contract them (KT Lemma~6.2). A
simple two-edge-connected multigraph on at least three bodies is settled
by B, applied to the whole graph, with no hypothesis at smaller
multigraphs. \Cref{sec:pencil-d-reduction} proves the cases in every
dimension. The reduction plays the role of the induction of KT
Theorem~4.9 (pp.~666--667). The two steps of that induction, contracting
a proper rigid subgraph and splitting off at a vertex of degree two,
have their counterparts in B.

\emph{The main-component induction.} At $d = 3$, we place each body at a
point of the plane, the picture $q$, and lift it to a point of $K^4$ by
a height (\cref{sec:pencil-main-component-route}). The lifted point is
the concurrency point of the body, and each edge gets the line through
the points of its ends. This configuration is a pencil realization when
the heights lie in a linear space $L(q)$, the lifting space. In
dimension $d$ a configuration has $d - 2$ heights, each in $L(q)$
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-condition-linear}). B shows that the general such
configuration has the deficiency rank in dimension $d$ at every graph
satisfying the standing hypotheses: finite, simple and connected, with
minimum degree at least two (\cref{def:pencil-x0-standing}). It is a
strong induction on the number of bodies, at a fixed $d$.
\Cref{sec:main-component} proves it at $d = 3$, and
\cref{sec:pencil-d-induction} to \cref{sec:pencil-d-coverage} in every
dimension. B concerns simple graphs only. Two parallel edges would get
the same hinge, the line through the points of their ends. Two bodies
joined by two edges would then have rank $5$ at $d = 3$, short of the
deficiency rank $6$ (\cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate}). This is why A
settles parallel edges itself.

Each step of B proves the claim at $G$ from the claim at none, one or
two graphs with fewer bodies (\cref{def:pencil-x0-reduces}). At $d = 3$
the steps are equal deficiencies, a cut vertex, a chain of bridges, a
cycle, open ears, and two contractions. An open ear has $k \ge 5$, three
or four interior bodies, or, at ends that are not adjacent and under
conditions on the deficiencies, two or one. A contraction collapses a
proper set $W$ of at least two bodies to one of its bodies. Either $W$
is planar-rigid, $\operatorname{def}_2(G[W]) = 0$, where the planar
deficiency $\operatorname{def}_2$ is the deficiency at $d = 2$
(\cref{def:D-deficiency}). Or $W$ is an additive core: it is rigid, and
the planar deficiencies of $G[W]$ and of the contraction add up to at
most that of $G$.

A chain is a path of bodies of degree two between two bodies of degree
at least three (\cref{def:pencil-x0-chain}). The structural coverage
shows that some step applies at every graph satisfying the standing
hypotheses (\cref{thm:pencil-x0-coverage}). It tests a chain of bridges,
a cut vertex, a cycle, equal deficiencies and a planar-rigid set first,
and then the chains. When no step at a chain applies, it finds an
additive core (\cref{thm:pencil-x0-theorem-s}). In dimension $d \ge 4$
the coverage takes the first five cases as at $d = 3$
(\cref{sec:pencil-d-coverage}). Then it embeds dimension three, when
$\operatorname{def}_3(G) = \operatorname{def}_d(G)$
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-x0-attains-of-le}). Otherwise it finds a chain with
many interior bodies or a core (\cref{thm:pencil-d-chain-or-core}).

Two steps of B assume the claim at no smaller graph: the cycle
(\cref{thm:pencil-x0-cycle}), and equal deficiencies. When
$\operatorname{def}_2(G) = \operatorname{def}_d(G)$, the general
configuration has the deficiency rank (\cref{cor:pencil-d-jj-flat}).
Indeed the flat configuration, with every height zero, already has it
over a main picture, one at which $\dim L(q)$ is least
(\cref{def:pencil-main-picture}). This rests on the dimension of the
lifting space at a general picture, $3 + \operatorname{def}_2(G)$
(\cref{thm:pencil-jj-equality}). Over the reals it follows from a
theorem of Jackson and Jord\'an~\cite[Theorem~7.1]{jacksonJordan2006pin},
through Whiteley's correspondence between scenes and the motions of
frameworks~\cite{whiteley1984}. We derive it instead from the case
$d = 2$ of KT Theorem~5.6, so that it holds over every infinite field.

Katoh and Tanigawa's Case~(I), the contraction of a proper rigid
subgraph, corresponds to the contractions of B. The contraction at an
additive core is KT Lemma~6.3, read on configurations
(\cref{sec:main-component-contract}). Their Cases~(II) and~(III),
splitting off at a vertex of degree two, correspond to the steps at an
open ear, which remove its interior bodies. Splitting off remains at $k
= 2$, $3$ and $4$. There the step also assumes the claim at $G$ with its
second interior body suppressed by a splitting-off
(\cref{def:pencil-x0-reduces}). No step asks whether the deficiency is
zero or positive, the condition that separates Case~(II) from
Case~(III). That distinction enters only where the coverage looks for a
core, in the proofs of \cref{thm:pencil-x0-theorem-s} and
\cref{thm:pencil-d-chain-or-core}.

Katoh and Tanigawa find a vertex of degree two by an edge count that
uses minimality (KT Lemmas~4.5 and~4.6, pp.~663--664). We count from
sparsity instead: $G$ is sparse when every set $X$ of at least two
bodies spans at most $\frac12(3|X| - 4)$ edges. A graph without a
planar-rigid set is sparse (\cref{lem:deficiency-sparse}). If it
satisfies the standing hypotheses, it then has at least four bodies of
degree two (\cref{lem:pencil-x0-sparse-count}). This is the degree count
by which Katoh and Tanigawa's proof of their Lemma~4.6 finds a vertex of
degree two. In dimension $d \ge 4$ the edges along the chains are
counted as in that proof (\cref{lem:pencil-d-chain-count}). In Katoh and
Tanigawa's proof the absence of a proper rigid subgraph gives a long
chain. Here, when every chain is short, a chain with at least two
interior bodies gives a core (\cref{lem:pencil-d-chain-core}).

\emph{The generic conjunct.} C proves the third claim by a strong
induction on the number of bodies, over the simple
nondegeneracy-feasible multigraphs (\cref{thm:pencil-d-generic}). It
lifts a general nondegenerate realization in dimension three to
dimension $d$ by $d - 3$ further coordinates (\cref{def:pencil-d-lift}).
At $d = 3$ the lift is the realization itself. On at most two bodies and
at a triangle every lift has the deficiency rank, and a disconnected
multigraph reduces to its parts. At a cut edge, at a body of degree one
and at a pendant triangle, the lifts of the smaller graphs extend
(\cref{sec:pencil-d-generic}). Without a planar-rigid set, the heights
at which a configuration of B has the deficiency rank give a lift at
that rank (\cref{lem:pencil-d-lift-no-rigid}). Otherwise the graph has a
pendant triangle, or a minimal planar-rigid set contains a body $x$ of
degree two whose neighbours are pencil hubs, bodies of degree at least
three (\cref{lem:pencil-d-good-ear}). The lifts of the graph without $x$
then extend across $x$ (\cref{lem:pencil-d-lift-ear}).

C is separate from B because the general configurations of B need not be
nondegenerate. When two pencil hubs have three common neighbours, as in
$K_{2,3}$, no configuration over a general picture is nondegenerate
(\cref{lem:pencil-x0-two-hubs-obstruction}). Yet $K_{2,3}$ is
nondegeneracy-feasible (\cref{sec:main-component-statements}). More
generally, no configuration over a main picture is nondegenerate when a
planar-rigid set contains two pencil hubs that are adjacent or have a
common neighbour (\cref{rem:pencil-x0-flatness}). Nor can C
take the place of B in A, as C concerns only the nondegeneracy-feasible
multigraphs. $K_4$ is simple and two-edge-connected but not
nondegeneracy-feasible (\cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate}). A needs an
adjacent-distinct realization of $K_4$, and B gives one.

\emph{How the parts depend on one another.} A uses B only at a simple
two-edge-connected multigraph, through its adjacent-distinct realization
in dimension $d$ (\cref{lem:pencil-d-distinct-statement}). C
uses the conclusion of B only at a graph without a planar-rigid set, in
the proof of \cref{thm:pencil-d-generic}. C also uses lemmas proved
alongside B that are not steps of B: the dimension of the lifting space,
the estimates on partitions, and several lemmas of
\cref{sec:main-component-statements}. Neither B nor C uses the induction
of A or its cases, and B uses nothing of C. The theorem in every
dimension combines A and C (\cref{cor:pencil-d-conditioned-pair}).

None of the three parts is an induction on $d$. This is a choice of
proof, not one forced by an example. The lower dimension enters in two
places. B embeds dimension three in dimension $d$ at the graphs with
$\operatorname{def}_3(G) = \operatorname{def}_d(G)$
(\cref{sec:pencil-d-stable}), and C lifts realizations from dimension
three. Within B the dimension is fixed, and all $d - 2$ heights are
chosen together. In the contraction step, for instance, the picture
moves along a curve, and every height moves with it
(\cref{sec:pencil-d-contract}).

Six observations explain the shape of B and C.

\emph{The heights recover a gap.} Let $G$ satisfy the standing
hypotheses. At a main picture $q$, the flat configuration has rank
$D(|V(G)| - 1) + 3 - \dim L(q)$ (\cref{thm:pencil-d-flat-rank}). At such
a picture $\dim L(q) = 3 + \operatorname{def}_2(G)$
(\cref{thm:pencil-jj-equality}). So the flat configuration falls short
of the deficiency rank by exactly $\operatorname{def}_2(G) -
\operatorname{def}_d(G)$, and the heights must recover this gap. When
the two deficiencies agree, there is no gap.

\emph{A configuration displaces the flat configuration along a motion.}
Let $d = 3$, let $q$ be an admissible picture, and let $z \in L(q)$. In
the configuration $(q, z)$ the points of the closed neighbourhood of
each body $v$ lie in a plane $\zeta = h_v(\xi, \eta)$, the panel of
$v$, where $h_v$ is an affine function
(\cref{lem:pencil-condition-linear}). In the flat configuration every
panel is the plane $\zeta = 0$. At an edge $uv$ the functions $h_u$ and
$h_v$ agree at $q_u$ and at $q_v$. So by the flat split the family
$(h_v)$ comes from a motion of the flat configuration
(\cref{lem:pencil-flat-split}). Over the reals, the points of the plane
that move with $v$ have the velocities $h_v$ normal to the plane in
this motion (\cref{sec:main-component-flat}). So for a scalar $t$ the
configuration $(q, tz)$ is the flat configuration with every panel
displaced by $t$ times its velocity. The panels of adjacent bodies
still meet in their hinge, the line through the points of the two
bodies. Every $t \neq 0$ gives the same rank
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-rank-scale-shift}), and $t = 0$ gives the flat
rank. In dimension $d$ each of the $d - 2$ heights comes from such a
motion, and displaces the panels in its own coordinate.

\emph{The rank depends on a space of heights.} Let $q$ be an admissible
picture, and let $\mathrm{Aff}(q) \subseteq L(q)$ be the space of the
globally affine heights, of dimension three
(\cref{lem:pencil-lifting-space-affine}). Let $z^1, \dots, z^{d-2}$ be
heights in $L(q)$, and let $W$ be the span of their classes in
$L(q)/\mathrm{Aff}(q)$. The rank of the configuration $(q, z)$ depends
only on $q$ and $W$. Indeed, two families of heights with the same $W$
differ by an invertible matrix and by globally affine heights, and
neither changes the rank (\cref{lem:pencil-d-rank-scale-shift}). At a
main picture of a graph satisfying the standing hypotheses, $W$ has
dimension at most $\min(d - 2, \operatorname{def}_2(G))$.

\emph{Bodies of degree two.} A body of degree two, whose closed
neighbourhood has three members, imposes no condition on the heights
(\cref{lem:pencil-three-points}). So the conditions that cut out $L(q)$
sit at the pencil hubs, on their closed neighbourhoods, which may
contain bodies of degree two. A body that is not a hub still carries a
condition of nondegeneracy, on the points of its closed neighbourhood
(\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}). The steps of B at an open ear remove
bodies of degree two, the interior bodies of the ear.

\emph{A planar-rigid set acts as one body.} Contraction at a
planar-rigid set $W$ assumes the claim only at the contraction, in which
$W$ is one body (\cref{cor:pencil-x0-contract-rigid}). Both deficiencies
of $G[W]$ vanish, so its general configuration attains by the case of
equal deficiencies. The points of a closed neighbourhood lie in the panel
of its body. Likewise, over a main picture the points of $W$ lie in one
plane, which is the panel of each body of $W$
(\cref{rem:pencil-x0-flatness}). The additive cores that the coverage
finds when there is no planar-rigid set are rigid, but not planar-rigid
(\cref{thm:pencil-x0-theorem-s}). In C, let $W_0$ be the minimal
planar-rigid set and $x$ the body of degree two of
\cref{lem:pencil-d-good-ear}. The rest $X = W_0 \setminus \{x\}$ has
planar deficiency one. At a general nondegenerate realization in
dimension three, every lift of the realization restricted to $G[X]$ is
trivial: one linear form on $K^4$ gives it at every body, as at a single
body (\cref{lem:pencil-d-ear-free}).

\emph{Room in larger dimensions.} Let $d \ge 4$, and let $G$ satisfy the
standing hypotheses and fail the first five cases of the coverage. Let
every chain of $G$ have at most $\ell$ interior bodies, where $3(\ell +
1) \le D$. A count of the edges along the chains then applies
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-chain-count}). With it, a chain with at least two
interior bodies gives a core (\cref{lem:pencil-d-chain-core}). When
every chain has one interior body, a core exists if
$\operatorname{def}_3(G) > 0$
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-core-positive-deficiency}). Otherwise
$\operatorname{def}_3(G) = \operatorname{def}_d(G) = 0$, and the
embedding applies (\cref{thm:pencil-d-chain-or-core}). So the larger
$D$, the longer the chains that the count settles. The coverage takes
$\ell = 2$ at $d = 4$, and $\ell = 3$ from $d = 5$ on. At $d = 3$ only
$\ell = 1$ is allowed. There the coverage uses the steps at chains with
one or two interior bodies (\cref{thm:pencil-x0-open-ear-one} and
\cref{thm:pencil-x0-open-ear-two-orbit}). In dimension $d \ge 4$ they
enter only through the embedding.

\subsection{Extensors through a point}
\label{sec:pencil-through-point}

Hinge-coplanarity is a containment condition: a screw element lies in
the panel with normal $n$ when it is the extensor of points of the
hyperplane $n^{\perp}$ (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}). We
dualize this to concurrency. A screw element passing through a point
replaces containment in the hyperplane by containment of the point in
the element's span: at $k = 2$ this says a line --- the extensor of two
points --- contains the point.

\begin{definition}[Extensor through a point]
  \label{def:extensor-through-point}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.ExtensorThroughPoint}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:screw-space, def:extensor}
  A screw element $C \in \bigwedge^{k} K^{k+2}$ \emph{passes through}
  the homogeneous point $q \in K^{k+2}$ when $C$ is the extensor of $k$
  points $p_1, \dots, p_k \in K^{k+2}$ whose span contains $q$.
\end{definition}

For $k \ge 2$ the zero element passes through every point (take every
$p_i = q$), so we treat the nonzero-ness of $C$ as a separate
hypothesis wherever the notion is used below, where $k = 2$. For
$k = 1$ the extensor of $q$ is $q$, and the zero element passes through
no nonzero point.

\subsection{Pencil realizations}
\label{sec:pencil-realization}

A pencil realization asks that, at every body, the incident hinges lie
in the body's panel and pass through one point of that panel. In
dimension three, the hinges at a body then lie in a flat pencil of
lines, Crapo and Whiteley's name for the lines through a point in a
plane. The extensors of a flat pencil form a vector space of dimension
two, spanned by those of any two of its
lines~\cite[\S3.5, p.~67]{crapoWhiteley1982}. So the hinges at a body
span at most two dimensions of the screw space. Conversely, three lines
have rank two only if they lie in a flat pencil (p.~56). In the
molecular reading
(\cref{sec:molecule-modelling-defs}) the panel is the plane of an atom's
bonds and the point is the atom's centre.

\begin{definition}[Pencil realization]
  \label{def:pencil-panel-realization}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.HasPencilPanelRealization}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:coplanar-panel-realization, def:extensor-through-point}
  A hinge-coplanar panel realization of a multigraph $G$
  (\cref{def:coplanar-panel-realization}), with normal assignment $v
  \mapsto n_v$, has a \emph{pencil realization} when it is given in
  addition a per-body nonzero homogeneous point $v \mapsto q_v \in
  K^{k+2}$, incident to its own panel ($q_v \cdot n_v = 0$). Every
  link's supporting extensor must then pass through the points of both
  its endpoints (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}). We call this
  requirement the \emph{pencil condition}.
\end{definition}

The incidence $q_v \cdot n_v = 0$ is forced at any body with a nonzero
hinge: the hinge's spanning points lie in $n_v^{\perp}$ and contain
$q_v$ in their span, so $q_v \in n_v^{\perp}$. We require it at every
body, isolated ones included, so that the normal and the point play
symmetric roles under the polarity below (\cref{sec:pencil-duality}).
Two hinges in one panel always pass through a common point of it
(\cref{lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent}). So at a body of degree at most
two, asking the hinges to pass through one point adds nothing to
hinge-coplanarity. The requirement first has content at a body with
three or more hinges.

\subsection{The polarity and self-duality}
\label{sec:pencil-duality}

The hinges at a body of a pencil realization are both coplanar and
concurrent. Tay and Whiteley note that concurrent hinges, as in models
of molecules, are ``the natural projective dual'' of coplanar ones, as
in flat architectural panels~\cite[p.~33]{tayWhiteley1984}. The
polarity $x \mapsto x^{\perp}$ of projective three-space takes a
molecular framework to a panel-hinge framework
(\cref{lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular}). This subsection shows that it
takes a pencil realization to a pencil realization, with the panels and
the points exchanged.

Work at grade $k = 2$ ($d = 3$) for the rest of this subsection, the
setting of \cref{lem:polarity-join}. Only in three-space does projective
duality take a hinge structure to a hinge structure, as Tay and Whiteley
observe (p.~34). Indeed, a nonzero hinge in dimension $d$ is the
extensor of $d - 1$ independent points of $K^{d+1}$. The orthogonal
complement of their span has dimension two, so the polarity takes the
hinge to a line, which is a hinge only when $d = 3$. In dimension three
the polarity sends the extensor of two homogeneous points
$v_0, v_1 \in K^4$ to the panel-support extensor $C(v_0,v_1)$
(\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}), over every field $K$: the
join-of-two-points $\leftrightarrow$ meet-of-two-panels identity. It is
a correlation, and correlations preserve the motions of a body-hinge
framework~\cite[\S3.6, p.~68]{crapoWhiteley1982}, as collineations do
(\cref{thm:projective-invariance}). The two transport lemmas below also
hold over every field $K$. They show that the polarity turns passing
through the point $q$ into lying in the panel with normal $q$, and lying
in that panel into passing through $q$.

\begin{lemma}[Passing through a point transports to lying in a panel]
  \label{lem:pencil-transport-through-to-in}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensorInPanel_screwComplementIso_of_extensorThroughPoint}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:extensor-through-point, def:genuine-hinge-realization,
        lem:polarity-join}
  If a screw element $C \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$ passes through the
  homogeneous point $q \in K^4$ (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}),
  then its polarity image lies in the panel with normal $q$
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:panel-support-extensor-join, def:panel-support-extensor}
  If $C = 0$ the image is $0$, which lies in every panel. Otherwise $C$
  is the extensor of an independent pair $v_0, v_1$ with $q$ in their
  span; the polarity (\cref{lem:polarity-join}) sends
  $C$ to the panel-support extensor $C(v_0,v_1)$
  (\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}), which by the point-join
  $\leftrightarrow$ panel-meet duality is the extensor of an
  independent pair $p_0', p_1'$ orthogonal to both $v_0$ and $v_1$
  (\cref{lem:panel-support-extensor-join} and the remark after it).
  Since $q$ lies in the span of $v_0, v_1$ and the dot product is
  linear, $p_0'$ and $p_1'$ are also orthogonal to $q$, so the image
  lies in the panel with normal $q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Lying in a panel transports to passing through a point]
  \label{lem:pencil-transport-in-to-through}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensorThroughPoint_screwComplementIso_of_extensorInPanel}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:extensor-through-point, def:genuine-hinge-realization,
        lem:polarity-join}
  If a screw element $C \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$ lies in the panel
  with normal $q \in K^4$ (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}), then
  its polarity image passes through $q$
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:panel-support-extensor-join, def:panel-support-extensor}
  If $C = 0$ the image is $0$, which passes through every point (take
  the constant family at $q$). Otherwise $C$ is the extensor of an
  independent pair $v_0, v_1$ both orthogonal to $q$; the polarity
  sends $C$ to the panel-support extensor $C(v_0,v_1)$
  (\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}), which is the extensor of an
  independent pair $p_0', p_1'$ orthogonal to both $v_0$ and $v_1$
  (\cref{lem:panel-support-extensor-join} and the remark after it).
  The orthogonal complement of $\{v_0,v_1\}$ is $2$-dimensional, hence
  equal to the span of the independent pair $p_0', p_1'$; as $q$ also
  lies in this complement, $q \in \mathrm{span}\{p_0',p_1'\}$, i.e.\ the
  image passes through $q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Self-duality of pencil realizations]
  \label{lem:pencil-self-dual}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasPencilPanelRealization_mapExtensor_screwComplementIso}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-panel-realization, lem:polarity-join,
        thm:projective-invariance}
  Over any field $K$, transporting a pencil realization $(F, n, q)$ of
  a multigraph $G$ along the polarity $\Lambda$ of
  \cref{lem:polarity-join} (field-general as a screw-space
  automorphism, \cref{thm:projective-invariance}) produces a pencil
  realization $(\Lambda F, q, n)$ of $G$, with the roles of the normal
  and the point exchanged.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:pencil-transport-through-to-in, lem:pencil-transport-in-to-through}
  The transported framework has the same multigraph and nonzero
  supporting extensors, since the polarity is a linear automorphism
  (\cref{thm:projective-invariance}). Its new normal assignment is $q$,
  each nonzero by hypothesis, and the incidence $n_v \cdot q_v = 0$
  read the other way gives $q_v \cdot n_v = 0$, so it is preserved.
  Let $C$ be the supporting extensor of a link $uw$. As $C$ passes
  through $q_u$ and $q_w$, its image lies in the panels with normals
  $q_u$ and $q_w$ (\cref{lem:pencil-transport-through-to-in}). As $C$
  lies in the panels with normals $n_u$ and $n_w$, its image passes
  through $n_u$ and $n_w$ (\cref{lem:pencil-transport-in-to-through}).
\end{proof}

We record this symmetry for its own sake: the proof of
\cref{thm:pencil-realization-theorem} does not use it. It is one reason why
\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization} allows the points of a link's two
endpoints to coincide: requiring them to be projectively distinct would
break the symmetry. \cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate} gives a second
reason, and imposes distinctness as a separate condition.

We have not found this self-duality stated. It follows a pattern of
Whiteley's: plane sheets joined at points, the jointed sheetworks, form
``a self-polar class of structures''~\cite[p.~344]{whiteley1987}. A
jointed sheetwork gives each sheet a plane and each joint a point, with
every joint on the sheets attached to it (Definition~4.1). Its polar
exchanges the sheets and the joints (Definition~4.3, p.~345). The panels
and the points of a pencil realization form a jointed sheetwork, in
which the panel of each body carries the points of its closed
neighbourhood (\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}). In the polar
sheetwork, the panel with normal $q_w$ carries the point $n_v$ when $w$
lies in the closed neighbourhood of $v$, that is, when $v$ lies in the
closed neighbourhood of $w$. So the polar sheetwork is the one formed by
the panels and the points of the polar realization $(\Lambda F, q, n)$.
Whiteley proves that a jointed sheetwork and each of its polars have
identical static properties (Theorem~4.1, p.~345). His theorem concerns the statics of
sheetworks, not of body-hinge frameworks. For pencil realizations the
corresponding fact is projective invariance
(\cref{thm:projective-invariance}): a pencil realization and its polar
have the same rank.

\subsection{Pencil cycles: degree-two concurrency is automatic}
\label{sec:pencil-cycle}

Katoh--Tanigawa's cycle realization (\cref{lem:cycle-realization},
KT Lemma~5.4) places a cycle of bodies with each hinge the meet of two
adjacent panels. Every body has degree two, and at a body of degree at
most two the concurrency requirement costs nothing: two lines lying in a
common plane always meet in a point of that plane. The following records
this projective fact in the containment model, so that the cycle
realization --- indeed any hinge-coplanar realization
(\cref{def:coplanar-panel-realization}) restricted to its degree-two
bodies --- already admits the per-body concurrency point a pencil
realization asks for. The same fact accounts for two features of the
nondegeneracy conditions in \cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate}. A pencil
hub there is a body of
degree at least three, since only there does passing through one point
constrain the hinges. At a body of degree two, whose two hinges pass
through a common point for free, a further condition keeps them from
coinciding. \cref{thm:pencil-reduction} does not call on the two pencil
realizations of a cycle built below; they give a direct example of the
same fact, on the cycle realization Katoh--Tanigawa already use.

\begin{lemma}[Two coplanar hinges share a concurrency point]
  \label{lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_concurrency_point_of_extensorInPanel_pair}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:genuine-hinge-realization, def:extensor-through-point}
  Let $n \in K^4$ be nonzero and let $C_1, C_2 \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$
  be nonzero screw elements each lying in the panel with normal $n$
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}). Then there is a nonzero point
  $q \in n^{\perp}$ through which both pass
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Each hinge is the extensor of an independent pair, spanning a
  $2$-dimensional subspace of the panel $n^{\perp}$, which has dimension
  $3$ in $K^4$. Two $2$-dimensional subspaces of a $3$-dimensional space
  meet in dimension at least $2 + 2 - 3 = 1$ (the modular law), so their
  intersection contains a nonzero point $q$. Lying in each hinge's span,
  $q$ passes through both hinges; and lying in $n^{\perp}$ it is
  orthogonal to $n$.
\end{proof}

The range $3 \le m \le 4$ is exactly where the shared-normal family
exists at $d = 3$ (\cref{lem:cycle-normals}), so the triangle and the
quadrilateral are both realized. Since every body of a cycle has degree
two, the concurrency fact above (\cref{lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent})
supplies each body's point directly, alongside the shared-normal
family's panel data, giving a pencil realization outright.

\begin{lemma}[Pencil realization of a cycle]
  \label{lem:cycle-pencil-realization}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_pencilPanelRealization_cycle}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cycle-data, def:pencil-panel-realization}
  Let $G$ be presented as a cycle of $m$ bodies (\cref{def:cycle-data})
  with $3 \le m \le 4$. Then $G$ has a pencil realization
  (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}) that is infinitesimally rigid on
  all of its bodies $V(G)$ (\cref{def:trivial-motions-rigid}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:cycle-normals, lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent, lem:cycle-realization-rigid,
        def:panel-support-extensor, def:genuine-hinge-realization, def:extensor-through-point,
        lem:panel-support-extensor-join}
  At grade $k = 2$ the cyclic shared-normal family
  (\cref{lem:cycle-normals}) supplies $m$ panel normals $n_0, \dots,
  n_{m-1}$ --- the range $3 \le m \le 4 = k + 2$ is exactly its
  hypothesis --- with each consecutive pair $n_i, n_{i+1}$ linearly
  independent. The $i$-th cycle edge is assigned the panel meet
  $C(n_i, n_{i+1})$ (\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}), nonzero and lying
  in both endpoint panels $n_i^{\perp}$, $n_{i+1}^{\perp}$
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}); every edge label off the cycle
  is given a fixed nonzero fallback, so all supporting extensors are
  nonzero. Body $i$ has exactly two incident hinges, $C(n_{i-1}, n_i)$ and
  $C(n_i, n_{i+1})$, both lying in the panel $n_i^{\perp}$; by
  \cref{lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent} they share a nonzero point
  $q_i \in n_i^{\perp}$, through which both pass
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}). Assign body $i$ the point $q_i$.
  Then each point is nonzero and incident to its own panel, and the
  edge $C(n_i, n_{i+1})$ passes through the points of both its endpoints:
  through $q_i$ as body $i$'s second hinge, and through $q_{i+1}$ as body
  $i+1$'s first hinge (its first incident hinge is $C(n_i, n_{i+1})$
  since $(i+1) - 1 = i$). The $m$ cyclic meets are linearly independent
  (\cref{lem:cycle-normals}), so the framework is infinitesimally rigid on
  the cycle's bodies, which are all of $V(G)$ (\cref{lem:cycle-realization-rigid}).
\end{proof}

The coplanar realization below is its corollary, discarding the
per-body concurrency point: a hinge-coplanar panel realization needs
only the panel data, which the pencil realization above already
supplies, built directly from the cyclic family of shared panel
normals rather than through the meet model of the original cycle
realization.

\begin{lemma}[Coplanar realization of a cycle]
  \label{lem:cycle-coplanar-realization}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_coplanarPanelRealization_cycle}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cycle-data, def:coplanar-panel-realization}
  Let $G$ be presented as a cycle of $m$ bodies (\cref{def:cycle-data})
  with $3 \le m \le 4$. Then $G$ has a hinge-coplanar panel realization
  (\cref{def:coplanar-panel-realization}) that is infinitesimally rigid on
  all of its bodies $V(G)$ (\cref{def:trivial-motions-rigid}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:cycle-pencil-realization}
  Immediate corollary of the pencil realization of the cycle
  (\cref{lem:cycle-pencil-realization}), discarding the per-body concurrency point: a pencil
  realization is in particular hinge-coplanar, and rigidity is unaffected by dropping a conjunct.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The two-pencil extension step}
\label{sec:pencil-extension}

Katoh--Tanigawa's induction extends a hinge-coplanar realization across
a re-added edge by supplying a supporting extensor in the meet of the
two endpoint panels (p.~670): for any two normals the common perp
$n_u^{\perp} \cap n_v^{\perp} \subseteq K^4$ has dimension at least
$4 - 2 = 2$, so it contains a nonzero hinge lying in both panels, with
no transversality of the normals needed. On the pencil condition the
re-added hinge must additionally pass through the concurrency point of
each endpoint. Where in the coplanar model a common hinge always
exists, the pencil model asks for one through two prescribed points as
well, and this constrains the two bodies: a hinge lying in both panels
and passing through both points confines each point to the common perp,
so each concurrency point must lie not only in its own panel but in the
other body's panel too. The following existence step assumes exactly
these two \emph{cross-incidences}.

\begin{lemma}[Two-pencil extension hinge]
  \label{lem:two-pencil-extension}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_extensor_two_pencils}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:genuine-hinge-realization, def:extensor-through-point}
  Let $n_u, n_v \in K^4$ be panel normals and $q_u, q_v \in K^4$
  concurrency points with $q_u$ incident to its own panel
  ($q_u \cdot n_u = 0$) and $q_v$ to its own ($q_v \cdot n_v = 0$),
  and suppose $q_u \ne 0$. If the two cross-incidences
  $q_u \cdot n_v = 0$ and $q_v \cdot n_u = 0$ hold --- each
  concurrency point lies in the other body's panel --- then there is a
  nonzero $C \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$ lying in both panels
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}) and passing through both points
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Under the four incidences both points lie in the common perp
  $W = n_u^{\perp} \cap n_v^{\perp}$, which has dimension at least $2$
  in $K^4$ regardless of whether the normals are independent. It
  suffices to exhibit a $2$-dimensional subspace of $W$ containing both
  points; its extensor is then a nonzero hinge in both panels through
  both points. If $q_u$ and $q_v$ are linearly independent their span
  is such a subspace. Otherwise $q_v$ lies on the line through $q_u$,
  and since $W$ has dimension at least $2$ while that line has dimension
  $1$, $W$ contains a vector $w$ independent from $q_u$; the span of
  $q_u$ and $w$ is a $2$-dimensional subspace of $W$ containing $q_u$,
  hence also $q_v$.
\end{proof}

The two cross-incidences are not only sufficient but necessary, so the
extension hinge exists precisely when the two bodies' concurrency points
are mutually panel-incident.

\begin{lemma}[Two-pencil extension biconditional]
  \label{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_extensor_two_pencils_iff,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.dotProduct_eq_zero_of_extensorInPanel_of_extensorThroughPoint}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:two-pencil-extension, def:genuine-hinge-realization,
        def:extensor-through-point}
  With the hypotheses of \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension} --- the points
  $q_u, q_v$ incident to their own panels and $q_u \ne 0$ --- the two
  cross-incidences $q_u \cdot n_v = 0$ and $q_v \cdot n_u = 0$ hold if and
  only if there is a nonzero screw element lying in both panels
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}) and passing through both points
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:two-pencil-extension, lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}
  The forward implication is \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension}. For the
  converse, suppose such a nonzero hinge $C$ exists. Two point pairs with the same nonzero
  $2$-extensor span the same plane (\cref{lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}), the converse of
  the point-pair proportionality of \cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union}.
  So the points through which $C$ passes and the points spanning it inside a
  panel span one and the same plane; since $C$ passes through $q_u$ and lies
  in the panel of $n_v$, the point $q_u$ lies in a span of vectors orthogonal
  to $n_v$, giving $q_u \cdot n_v = 0$, and symmetrically $q_v \cdot n_u = 0$.
\end{proof}

These cross-incidences are the chapter's first condition that the
coplanar model's extension step does not need, and three later
arguments turn on them. Katoh--Tanigawa reach a graph that is not
minimal by adding edges to a realization of a minimal spanning subgraph.
For pencil realizations the cross-incidences of the added edges cannot
always be met, so we run the induction on every multigraph
(\cref{sec:pencil-reduction}). The cut-edge case meets the
cross-incidences of its crossing edge by first repositioning one side
along a projective automorphism (\cref{lem:pencil-cut-nondegeneracy};
in every dimension, \cref{lem:pencil-d-cut-reposition}).
At $K_4$ the necessity direction
of \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff} forces every vertex's panel
normal into a single hyperplane, so no simple realization of $K_4$ can
give all four vertices independent panel normals
(\cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate}).

\subsection{Reduction on all spanning multigraphs}
\label{sec:pencil-reduction}

This subsection states the induction behind
\cref{thm:pencil-realization-theorem}. Katoh--Tanigawa's induction runs
over minimal graphs, those from which no edge can be deleted without
changing the deficiency (\cref{thm:minimal-kdof-reduction-all-k}). They reach any
other graph by realizing a minimal spanning subgraph with the same
deficiency and adding the remaining edges, each with a hinge in the meet
of its two panels (\cref{thm:theorem-55-6-multigraph}). The added edges
need not raise the rank, since the subgraph already attains the
deficiency rank.

For pencil realizations this step fails, and the failure lies in the
smaller graph. An added edge needs its two cross-incidences
(\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}), and imposing them can lower the
rank of the smaller graph. Take $K_4$ and a $4$-cycle in it, both of
deficiency $0$. A pencil realization of the $4$-cycle has rank $14$ plus
the dimension of the span of its four hinges, so it attains its
deficiency rank $18$ only when the hinges are linearly independent. The cross-incidences
of the two diagonals, with those the cycle edges satisfy already, put all
four points in every panel, so the points span at most a plane. If they
span a plane, every panel is that plane, and the hinges lie in the
three-dimensional space of its lines. If they span a line, at least two
edges of the cycle join bodies with different points, and both of their
hinges are that line. If they coincide, all four hinges pass through one
point. In each case the hinges span at most three dimensions, so no
pencil realization of the $4$-cycle at its deficiency rank admits the
diagonals.

We therefore run the induction on every multigraph. Its cases are
structural: a loop, at most two bodies, a cut edge, parallel edges, and
a simple two-edge-connected multigraph (\cref{thm:pencil-reduction}).
None of them asks for a proper rigid subgraph or a vertex of degree two.
So the reduction plays the role only of the outer induction of KT
Theorem~4.9 (pp.~666--667), whose two steps, contracting a proper rigid
subgraph and splitting off at a vertex of degree two, have their
counterparts in the main-component induction that settles the last case
(\cref{sec:pencil-roadmap}). The statement the induction proves is the
following.

\begin{definition}[Pencil realization at the deficiency rank]
  \label{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.HasPencilRealization}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-panel-realization, def:D-deficiency}
  A \emph{pencil realization at the deficiency rank} of a multigraph $G$ is
  a pencil realization $(F, n, q)$ of $G$
  (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}) whose rigidity rows span dimension
  \[
    \dim \operatorname{span}(\text{rigidity rows of } F)
      = D(|V(G)| - 1) - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G),
  \]
  the target of \cref{thm:theorem-55-6-multigraph} restricted to the pencil
  condition.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Reduction on all spanning multigraphs]
  \label{thm:pencil-reduction}
  \lean{Graph.pencil_reduction}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cut-edges-2ec}
  A property $P$ of multigraphs that is closed under five cases holds of
  every nonempty multigraph. The five cases are: \textup{(i)} $G$ has a
  loop (delete it and reinsert it); \textup{(ii)} $G$ is loopless with
  $|V(G)| \le 2$ (base case); \textup{(iii)} $G$ is loopless, $|V(G)| \ge
  3$, and not two-edge-connected (cut-edge decomposition,
  \cref{def:cut-edges-2ec}); \textup{(iv)} $G$ is loopless,
  two-edge-connected and not simple, and $|V(G)| \ge 3$; \textup{(v)}
  $G$ is simple and two-edge-connected, and $|V(G)| \ge 3$. Case (i) is
  handed the property at every nonempty multigraph smaller than $G$ in
  the lexicographic order on $(|V(G)|, |E(G)|)$. Cases (iii) and (iv)
  are handed it at every nonempty multigraph on fewer vertices than $G$;
  case (v) is handed nothing.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Strong induction on $(|V(G)|, |E(G)|)$, lexicographically. Its
  hypothesis at $G$ gives the property at every smaller nonempty
  multigraph, in particular at every one on fewer vertices. Deleting a
  loop keeps $|V(G)|$ and lowers $|E(G)|$, so it gives a smaller
  multigraph. A loopless multigraph with at least three vertices falls
  under exactly one of cases (iii), (iv) and~(v).
\end{proof}

The cases are proved in every dimension (\cref{sec:pencil-d-reduction}):
at a loop (\cref{lem:pencil-d-loop-case}), on at most two bodies
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-base-case}) and at a cut edge
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-cut-case}), each with hinges in two pencils
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-two-pencil-extension}). Case~(iv) contracts two
parallel edges, as Katoh and Tanigawa do
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-nonsimple-case}; KT Lemma~6.2). Case~(v) is settled
by an adjacent-distinct realization of the whole multigraph in dimension
$d$ (\cref{lem:pencil-d-distinct-statement}), which rests on the argument
of \cref{sec:main-component}. Both arguments contract proper rigid
subgraphs, and contraction leaves the deficiency unchanged
(\cref{lem:pencil-contraction-deficiency}).

At a cut crossed by one edge, the two sides are realized separately, and
the two cross-incidences of the crossing edge need not hold
(\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}). The next lemma gives, in dimension
three, a projective automorphism after which they hold once one side is
moved by it. Its form in every dimension, with the normals and the points
exchanged, is \cref{lem:pencil-d-cut-reposition}.

\begin{lemma}[Existence of a repositioning automorphism]
  \label{lem:pencil-cut-nondegeneracy}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_reposition_cross_incidences,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_contragredient_linearEquiv,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_perp_linearIndependent}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}
  Fix the panel/point data of the two sides at a crossing edge $uv$: normal
  $n_1(u)$ and point $pt_1(u) \ne 0$ on the $V_1$ side, normal $n_2(v)$ and
  point $pt_2(v) \ne 0$ on the $V_2$ side. Then there is a linear
  automorphism $g$ of $K^4$ with a contragredient $h$ ($\langle g x, h
  y\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle$ for the standard pairing) meeting the two
  cross-incidences of \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff} once the $V_2$
  side is transported by $(g, h)$: $\langle pt_1(u), h\, n_2(v)\rangle = 0$
  and $\langle g\, pt_2(v), n_1(u)\rangle = 0$; moreover the transported
  point $g\, pt_2(v)$ is projectively distinct from $pt_1(u)$, which the
  construction supplies for free.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Explicit, over any field. Every automorphism of $K^4$ has a
  contragredient, the inverse-transpose. Pick $a \in n_1(u)^\perp$ with
  $\{a, pt_1(u)\}$ independent and $b \in n_2(v)^\perp$ with $\{b,
  pt_2(v)\}$ independent, possible because each hyperplane $\perp$ is at
  least $3$-dimensional and so is not contained in a line. Let $g$ be the
  frame automorphism sending $pt_2(v) \mapsto a$ and $b \mapsto pt_1(u)$.
  Then $g\, pt_2(v) = a \in n_1(u)^\perp$ is the second incidence, and $g\,
  b = pt_1(u)$ with the contragredient identity turns the first into $b \in
  n_2(v)^\perp$. The distinctness clause is the independence of $\{a,
  pt_1(u)\}$ chosen at the start, read through $g\, pt_2(v) = a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Deficiency is unchanged by contracting a proper rigid subgraph]
  \label{lem:pencil-contraction-deficiency}
  \lean{Graph.rigidContract_deficiency_eq}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:rigid-subgraph, lem:contract-deficiency-conservation,
    def:rigid-contraction}
  Let $H$ be a proper rigid subgraph of $G$. Contracting $H$ to a single
  vertex leaves the deficiency unchanged,
  $\operatorname{def}(\widetilde{G / E(H)}) = \operatorname{def}(\tilde
  G)$, without assuming $G$ minimal.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:reduction-measure, lem:two-edge-conn}
  The matroid-contraction rank identity and the rank/deficiency bridge
  behind \cref{lem:contract-deficiency-conservation} already use no
  minimality; only the base/fiber-meeting half of the corresponding
  minimality-tracking statement did, and that half is not needed here.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Nondegenerate realizations and the conditioned pair}
\label{sec:pencil-nondegenerate}

Katoh and Tanigawa's induction statement asks for more than the rank.
When the graph is simple, their realization must also be nonparallel,
that is, no two of its panels are parallel (KT p.~668; KT Theorem~5.5,
p.~670). This subsection defines a pencil analogue, the nondegenerate
pencil realization, and strengthens the statement of
\cref{thm:pencil-realization-theorem} to include it. The strengthened
statement, the conditioned pair, asks for a nondegenerate realization at
the deficiency rank under two conditions on the graph. We show that neither condition can be dropped. Throughout, $k = 2$, so panel normals and concurrency
points lie in $K^4$.

The pencil condition constrains the hinges at a body only when the body
has three or more of them (\cref{sec:pencil-realization}). The next
definition calls such a body a hub. It asks for independence among the
normals and among the points around each body.

\begin{definition}[Nondegenerate pencil realization, feasibility]
  \label{def:pencil-nondegenerate}
  \lean{Graph.PencilHub, Graph.closedHubNbhd, Graph.closedNbhd,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.IsNondegPencilRealization,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.PencilNondegFeasible}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-panel-realization}
  A vertex of a multigraph is a \emph{pencil hub} when it has degree at
  least three. The \emph{closed hub-neighbourhood} of a vertex $v$ is the
  set of pencil hubs among $v$ itself and its neighbours, and the
  \emph{closed neighbourhood} of $v$ is $v$ together with all of its
  neighbours, hub or not. A pencil realization of $G$
  (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}) is \emph{nondegenerate} when the
  two concurrency points of every link are projectively distinct; at every
  vertex, the panel normals of its closed hub-neighbourhood are linearly
  independent; and at every non-hub vertex, the concurrency points of its
  closed neighbourhood are linearly independent. A multigraph is
  \emph{nondegeneracy-feasible} when it has a nondegenerate pencil
  realization.
\end{definition}

\begin{fmlnote}
  The five pins are the five terms defined, in order: pencil hubs, the
  closed hub-neighbourhood, the closed neighbourhood, nondegenerate
  pencil realizations and nondegeneracy-feasibility.
\end{fmlnote}

The first condition makes the hinge of every link the line through the
points of its two ends (\cref{lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}). The
other two are limited by the incidences of a pencil realization. The hinge
of an edge $vw$ is nonzero, lies in the panel of $w$ and passes through
$q_v$, so $q_v \cdot n_w = 0$ (\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}).
Thus the normals of $v$ and its neighbours lie in the three-dimensional
space $q_v^{\perp}$, and their points lie in the panel $n_v^{\perp}$,
also of dimension three. Hence the normals of a closed
hub-neighbourhood are independent only if it has at most three members. At $K_4$ every body is
a hub and every closed hub-neighbourhood has four members, so $K_4$ is not
nondegeneracy-feasible. Likewise, the points of a closed neighbourhood are
independent only if it has at most three members, which rules out every
hub of a simple graph. The third condition is asked only at bodies that
are not hubs. At such a body it follows from the first condition and
$q_v \neq 0$, unless the body has two distinct neighbours. There it asks
that the two hinges at the body be distinct lines. The first two
conditions do not imply this. The path on three bodies has a pencil
realization whose three points are distinct but collinear, and both of its
hinges are then the same line.

In the next definition, generic names only the property stated, not an
algebraic independence of coordinates.

\begin{definition}[Generic pencil realization]
  \label{def:pencil-generic-motive}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.HasGenericPencilRealization}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-nondegenerate, def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}
  A multigraph $G$ \emph{has a generic pencil realization} when it has a
  nondegenerate pencil realization (\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate})
  whose rigidity rows attain the deficiency-rank target of
  \cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}.
\end{definition}

Keeping only the first condition of \cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate} gives
a weaker notion, which every simple multigraph with at least one body
satisfies at the deficiency rank (\cref{thm:pencil-realization-theorem}). We do not
add this condition to \cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}, for two reasons.
It would break the symmetry noted after \cref{lem:pencil-self-dual}. Also,
some multigraphs would have no pencil realization at the deficiency rank.
Two bodies joined by two edges have deficiency rank $D = 6$, which needs
two independent hinges. At distinct points both hinges are the line
through the two points, and the rank is $5$.

\begin{definition}[Adjacent-distinct pencil realization]
  \label{def:pencil-distinct-motive}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.HasDistinctPencilRealization}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis, def:pencil-nondegenerate}
  A multigraph $G$ \emph{has an adjacent-distinct pencil realization}
  when it has a pencil realization at the deficiency rank
  (\cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}) in which the two concurrency points
  of every link are projectively distinct.
\end{definition}

A generic pencil realization is adjacent-distinct, and an
adjacent-distinct one is in particular a pencil realization at the
deficiency rank.

The strengthened statement asks for a generic realization under two
conditions. Simplicity alone, the condition of KT Theorem~5.5, is not
enough, since $K_4$ is simple and not nondegeneracy-feasible.
Nondegeneracy-feasibility alone is not enough either. Two bodies joined
by two edges are nondegeneracy-feasible. Neither is a hub, so it suffices
to take two distinct panels, two distinct points on their common line, and
that line as both hinges. By the count above, none of their nondegenerate
realizations attains the deficiency rank. So the statement asks for both
conditions.

\begin{definition}[The conditioned pair]
  \label{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.PencilPair}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-nondegenerate, def:pencil-generic-motive,
        def:pencil-distinct-motive, def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}
  A multigraph $G$ satisfies the \emph{conditioned pair} at $d$ when it
  has a pencil realization at the deficiency rank
  (\cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}); when, if it is simple, it has an
  adjacent-distinct one (\cref{def:pencil-distinct-motive}); and when, if
  it is both simple and nondegeneracy-feasible
  (\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}), it has a generic one
  (\cref{def:pencil-generic-motive}).
\end{definition}
\begin{fmlnote}
  \label{fmlnote:pencil-conditioned-pair}
  In the Lean, this definition and \cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis},
  \cref{def:pencil-generic-motive} and \cref{def:pencil-distinct-motive}
  take the dimension $d$ of the deficiency (\cref{def:D-deficiency}, with
  $D = \binom{d+1}{2}$), which the formalization calls $n$, as an
  argument, while their realizations stay at $k = 2$, where the target has
  $D = 6$. The theorems that use them take $d = 3$, where the two agree.
\end{fmlnote}

\subsection{Lifting planar pictures, and the theorem}
\label{sec:pencil-main-component-route}

This subsection sketches the idea of \cref{sec:main-component}. It then
proves the pencil realization theorem, as the case $d = 3$ of the
conditioned pair in every dimension
(\cref{cor:pencil-d-conditioned-pair}). The road map
(\cref{sec:pencil-roadmap}) shows where the idea enters that proof.

The idea of \cref{sec:main-component} is to build pencil realizations
by lifting planar pictures.
Place each body $v$ at a point $q_v = (x_v, y_v)$ of the plane, with
adjacent bodies at distinct points and no closed neighbourhood on a
line. Such a picture is admissible (\cref{def:pencil-admissible-picture}).
Lift $v$ to the point $p_v = (x_v, y_v, z_v, 1)$ of $K^4$ at a height
$z_v$ (\cref{def:pencil-configuration}). Take $p_v$ as the concurrency
point of $v$, and the line through $p_u$ and $p_v$ as the hinge of an
edge $uv$. Every hinge then passes through the points of its two ends.
The hinges at $v$ lie in a common panel exactly when the points of the
closed neighbourhood of $v$ lie in a plane. So the lifted configuration
is a pencil realization when every closed neighbourhood is coplanar.
Over a fixed picture $q$ this is a linear condition on the heights
(\cref{lem:pencil-condition-linear}), and the heights that satisfy it
form a linear space $L(q)$, the lifting space
(\cref{def:pencil-lifting-space}). This is the setting of scene
analysis. The heights in $L(q)$ are those of the scenes over $q$ of the
incidence structure whose faces are the closed neighbourhoods of $G$
(\cref{sec:main-component-carrier}).

Over the admissible pictures at which $\dim L(q)$ is least, these
configurations form a vector bundle. Its closure is the main component
of the configuration space (\cref{rem:pencil-x0-main-component}). In
\cref{sec:main-component} we show that, at every simple connected
multigraph whose bodies all have degree at least two, a general
configuration of the main component has the deficiency rank
(\cref{thm:pencil-x0-coverage}). The proof is a strong induction
on the number of bodies. A lifted configuration at the deficiency rank
is an adjacent-distinct pencil realization, in every dimension
(\cref{lem:pencil-d-config-realization}). Nondegenerate realizations do
not follow in this way, as the road map explains at $K_{2,3}$.

\begin{theorem}[The pencil realization theorem]
  \label{thm:pencil-realization-theorem}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.pencil_realization_theorem,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.pencilPair_of_nonempty}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}
  Let $K$ be an infinite field. Every multigraph on the whole ambient
  body set satisfies the conditioned pair at $d = 3$
  (\cref{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}): it has a pencil realization at
  the deficiency rank, with adjacent concurrency points distinct when it
  is simple, and a generic one when it is also nondegeneracy-feasible.
  More generally, so does every multigraph with at least one body.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{cor:pencil-d-conditioned-pair, lem:pencil-d-rank-agrees-three,
        lem:pencil-d-nondegenerate-three}
  The second claim is the case $d = 3$ of
  \cref{cor:pencil-d-conditioned-pair}. At $d = 3$, a pencil realization
  at the deficiency rank and an adjacent-distinct one in the sense of
  \cref{sec:pencil-d} are those of \cref{sec:pencil}
  (\cref{lem:pencil-d-rank-agrees-three}), and so are
  nondegeneracy-feasibility and a generic realization
  (\cref{lem:pencil-d-nondegenerate-three}). So the three conjuncts of the
  corollary at $d = 3$ are those of the conditioned pair. A multigraph on
  the whole ambient body set has a body, as that set is nonempty, so the
  first claim is a case of the second.
\end{proof}
