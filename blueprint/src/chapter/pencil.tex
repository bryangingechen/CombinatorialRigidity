\section{Pencil realizations: hinges concurrent within a common panel}
\label{sec:pencil}

In dimension three, this chapter and \cref{sec:main-component}
strengthen KT Theorem~5.6 in the hinge-coplanar model of
\cref{sec:molecular-coplanar-multigraph}. A pencil realization gives
each body, besides its panel, a point of that panel, through which every
hinge at the body passes (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}). The
hinges at a body then form a pencil of lines through a point in a plane.
We show that, over every infinite field, every spanning multigraph $G$
has a pencil realization of rank
$6(|V(G)| - 1) - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$, the deficiency rank of
\cref{thm:theorem-55-6-multigraph}. If $G$ is simple, the realization
can be chosen with the points of adjacent bodies projectively distinct.
If moreover $G$ has a nondegenerate pencil realization
(\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}), it has one of that rank. These claims
make up \cref{thm:pencil-conjecture}. Besides
\cref{sec:molecular-coplanar-multigraph}, the reader needs the
deficiency and rigid subgraphs of \cref{sec:molecular-deficiency}.

\Cref{sec:pencil-through-point} and \cref{sec:pencil-realization} give
the definitions. \Cref{sec:pencil-base} places the two independent hinges
of Katoh and Tanigawa's base case (KT Lemma~5.3) through one point of
their common panel. \Cref{sec:pencil-extension} gives an added edge a
hinge through the points of both its ends. Such a hinge exists only when
the point of each end lies in the panel of the other
(\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}). These conditions can fail at the
edges that Katoh and Tanigawa add to a realization of a minimal spanning
subgraph. So we induct over every multigraph, not only over the minimal
ones (\cref{thm:pencil-reduction}). \Cref{sec:pencil-reduction}
states this induction and proves its cases at a loop, on at most two
bodies and at a cut edge. \Cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate} adds the
distinct points and the nondegenerate realizations to the induction
statement. Katoh and Tanigawa's induction statement likewise asks for
nonparallel realizations (KT Theorem~5.5). The cases at a loop, on at
most two bodies and at a cut edge hold for the strengthened statement
too, so it follows from the two remaining cases
(\cref{thm:pencil-conditional-realization-pair}).
\Cref{sec:pencil-main-component-route} proves these two cases,
where $G$ has a proper rigid subgraph and where it is two-edge-connected
without one. At a simple two-edge-connected multigraph both follow from
two statements (\cref{def:pencil-main-component-statements}), which
\cref{sec:main-component} proves by lifting pictures of $G$ in the plane.

Three parts of the chapter are not used in the proof of
\cref{thm:pencil-conjecture}. \Cref{sec:pencil-duality} shows that the
polarity of \cref{lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular} takes a pencil
realization to one with the panel normals and the points exchanged.
This chapter uses the polarity nowhere else. \Cref{sec:pencil-cycle}
realizes the cycles on three and four bodies as pencil realizations.
\Cref{sec:pencil-girth-chain} derives the shape of a simple
two-edge-connected multigraph with no proper rigid subgraph around a
body of degree two.

\subsection{Extensors through a point}
\label{sec:pencil-through-point}

Hinge-coplanarity is a containment condition: a screw element lies in
the panel with normal $n$ when it is the extensor of points of the
hyperplane $n^{\perp}$ (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}). We
dualize this to concurrency. A screw element passing through a point
replaces containment in the hyperplane by containment of the point in
the element's span: at $k = 2$ this says a line --- the extensor of two
points --- contains the point.

\begin{definition}[Extensor through a point]
  \label{def:extensor-through-point}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.ExtensorThroughPoint}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:rigidity-matrix, def:extensor}
  A screw element $C \in \bigwedge^{k} K^{k+2}$ \emph{passes through}
  the homogeneous point $q \in K^{k+2}$ when $C$ is the extensor of $k$
  points $p_1, \dots, p_k \in K^{k+2}$ whose span contains $q$.
\end{definition}

The zero element passes through every point (take every $p_i = q$), so
we treat the nonzero-ness of $C$ as a separate hypothesis wherever the
notion is used below.

\subsection{Pencil realizations}
\label{sec:pencil-realization}

A pencil realization asks that, at every body, the incident hinges lie
in the body's panel and pass through one point of that panel. This is
the classical picture of a pencil of lines through a point in a plane.
In the molecular reading (\cref{sec:molecule-modelling-defs}) the panel
is the plane of an atom's bonds and the point is the atom's centre.

\begin{definition}[Pencil realization]
  \label{def:pencil-panel-realization}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.HasPencilPanelRealization}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:coplanar-panel-realization, def:extensor-through-point}
  A hinge-coplanar panel realization of a multigraph $G$
  (\cref{def:coplanar-panel-realization}), with normal assignment $v
  \mapsto n_v$, has a \emph{pencil realization} when it is given in
  addition a per-body nonzero homogeneous point $v \mapsto q_v \in
  K^{k+2}$, incident to its own panel ($q_v \cdot n_v = 0$). Every
  link's supporting extensor must then pass through the points of both
  its endpoints (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}). We call this
  requirement the \emph{pencil condition}.
\end{definition}

The incidence $q_v \cdot n_v = 0$ is forced at any body with a nonzero
hinge: the hinge's spanning points lie in $n_v^{\perp}$ and contain
$q_v$ in their span, so $q_v \in n_v^{\perp}$. We require it at every
body, isolated ones included, so that the normal and the point play
symmetric roles under the polarity below (\cref{sec:pencil-duality}).
Two hinges in one panel always pass through a common point of it
(\cref{lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent}). So at a body of degree at most
two, asking the hinges to pass through one point adds nothing to
hinge-coplanarity. The requirement first has content at a body with
three or more hinges.

\subsection{The polarity and self-duality}
\label{sec:pencil-duality}

Work at grade $k = 2$ ($d = 3$) for the rest of this subsection, the
setting of \cref{lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular}. There the polarity
sends the extensor of two homogeneous points $v_0, v_1 \in K^4$ to the
panel-support extensor $C(v_0,v_1)$ (\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}):
the join-of-two-points $\leftrightarrow$ meet-of-two-panels identity,
here in its full generality rather than only for the homogenized poles
of a molecular framework. \cref{lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular} itself
fixes $K = \R$. The polarity and the two transport lemmas below instead
hold over an arbitrary field $K$. The two lemmas show that the polarity
turns passing through the point $q$ into lying in the panel with normal
$q$, and lying in that panel into passing through $q$.

\begin{lemma}[Passing through a point transports to lying in a panel]
  \label{lem:pencil-transport-through-to-in}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensorInPanel_screwComplementIso_of_extensorThroughPoint}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:extensor-through-point, def:genuine-hinge-realization,
        lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular}
  If a screw element $C \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$ passes through the
  homogeneous point $q \in K^4$ (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}),
  then its polarity image lies in the panel with normal $q$
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union, def:panel-support-extensor}
  If $C = 0$ the image is $0$, which lies in every panel. Otherwise $C$
  is the extensor of an independent pair $v_0, v_1$ with $q$ in their
  span; the polarity (\cref{lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular}) sends
  $C$ to the panel-support extensor $C(v_0,v_1)$
  (\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}), which by the point-join
  $\leftrightarrow$ panel-meet duality
  (\cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union}) is the extensor of
  an independent pair $p_0', p_1'$ orthogonal to both $v_0$ and $v_1$.
  Since $q$ lies in the span of $v_0, v_1$ and the dot product is
  linear, $p_0'$ and $p_1'$ are also orthogonal to $q$, so the image
  lies in the panel with normal $q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Lying in a panel transports to passing through a point]
  \label{lem:pencil-transport-in-to-through}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensorThroughPoint_screwComplementIso_of_extensorInPanel}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:extensor-through-point, def:genuine-hinge-realization,
        lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular}
  If a screw element $C \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$ lies in the panel
  with normal $q \in K^4$ (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}), then
  its polarity image passes through $q$
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union, def:panel-support-extensor}
  If $C = 0$ the image is $0$, which passes through every point (take
  the constant family at $q$). Otherwise $C$ is the extensor of an
  independent pair $v_0, v_1$ both orthogonal to $q$; the polarity
  sends $C$ to the panel-support extensor $C(v_0,v_1)$
  (\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}), which by
  \cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union} is the extensor of
  an independent pair $p_0', p_1'$ orthogonal to both $v_0$ and $v_1$.
  The orthogonal complement of $\{v_0,v_1\}$ is $2$-dimensional, hence
  equal to the span of the independent pair $p_0', p_1'$; as $q$ also
  lies in this complement, $q \in \mathrm{span}\{p_0',p_1'\}$, i.e.\ the
  image passes through $q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Self-duality of pencil realizations]
  \label{lem:pencil-self-dual}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasPencilPanelRealization_mapExtensor_screwComplementIso}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-panel-realization, lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular,
        thm:projective-invariance}
  Over any field $K$, transporting a pencil realization $(F, n, q)$ of
  a multigraph $G$ along the polarity $\Lambda$ of
  \cref{lem:panel-hinge-dual-molecular} (field-general as a screw-space
  automorphism, \cref{thm:projective-invariance}) produces a pencil
  realization $(\Lambda F, q, n)$ of $G$, with the roles of the normal
  and the point exchanged.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:pencil-transport-through-to-in, lem:pencil-transport-in-to-through}
  The transported framework has the same multigraph and nonzero
  supporting extensors, since the polarity is a linear automorphism
  (\cref{thm:projective-invariance}). Its new normal assignment is $q$,
  each nonzero by hypothesis, and the incidence $n_v \cdot q_v = 0$
  read the other way gives $q_v \cdot n_v = 0$, so it is preserved.
  Let $C$ be the supporting extensor of a link $uw$. As $C$ passes
  through $q_u$ and $q_w$, its image lies in the panels with normals
  $q_u$ and $q_w$ (\cref{lem:pencil-transport-through-to-in}). As $C$
  lies in the panels with normals $n_u$ and $n_w$, its image passes
  through $n_u$ and $n_w$ (\cref{lem:pencil-transport-in-to-through}).
\end{proof}

We record this symmetry for its own sake: the proof of
\cref{thm:pencil-conjecture} does not use it. It is one reason why
\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization} allows the points of a link's two
endpoints to coincide: requiring them to be projectively distinct would
break the symmetry. \cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate} gives a second
reason, and imposes distinctness as a separate condition.

\subsection{The coincident-panel pencil pair}
\label{sec:pencil-base}

This subsection supplies the pencil condition at the base case of
\cref{thm:pencil-reduction}: every loopless multigraph on at most two
vertices (\cref{lem:pencil-base-case}). Katoh--Tanigawa's two-vertex
base realization of a parallel edge pair
(\cref{lem:theorem-55-base-producer-parallel}, KT Lemma~5.3, p.~669)
places both hinges in a single shared panel $n^{\perp}$ with two
linearly independent supporting extensors
(\cref{lem:extensor-pair-in-panel}), which fill the whole screw space
$K^6$ and make the two bodies rigid. On the pencil condition the same
construction supplies a common concurrency point at no extra cost: two
lines of a flat pencil already share the point of the pencil.

\begin{lemma}[Two independent extensors through a common point of a panel]
  \label{lem:extensor-pair-through-point}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_linearIndependent_extensor_pair_through_point}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:extensor-through-point, def:genuine-hinge-realization}
  For any normal $n \in K^4$ there is a nonzero point $q \in n^{\perp}$
  and two screw elements $C, C' \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$, each lying
  in the panel with normal $n$ (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization})
  and passing through $q$ (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}), whose
  $2$-extensors are linearly independent.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:extensor-pair-in-panel}
  The panel $n^{\perp}$ has dimension at least $3$ in $K^4$, so it
  contains three linearly independent vectors $q, a, b$. Take the two
  pencil lines $q \vee a$ and $q \vee b$ through $q$: both lie in
  $n^{\perp}$ and contain $q$ in their span, so each lies in the panel
  and passes through $q$. Their extensors are linearly independent
  because $\{q, a, b\}$ is --- the shared-vector case of the
  independence of extensors on two distinct point subsets behind
  \cref{lem:extensor-pair-in-panel}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Two independent extensors through a prescribed point of a panel]
  \label{lem:extensor-pair-through-given-point}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_linearIndependent_extensor_pair_through_given_point}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:extensor-through-point, def:genuine-hinge-realization}
  For any normal $n \in K^4$ and any nonzero point $q \in n^{\perp}$, there
  are two screw elements $C, C' \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$, each lying in
  the panel with normal $n$ (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}) and
  passing through $q$ (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}), whose
  $2$-extensors are linearly independent.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:extensor-pair-in-panel}
  As for \cref{lem:extensor-pair-through-point}, with $q$ given in
  advance. The panel $n^{\perp}$ has
  dimension at least $3$ in $K^4$, so $q$ extends to a linearly
  independent triple $q, y, z$ in $n^{\perp}$. The two pencil lines
  $q \vee y$ and $q \vee z$ through $q$ both lie in $n^{\perp}$ and
  contain $q$ in their span, so each lies in the panel and passes through
  $q$; their extensors are linearly independent because $\{q, y, z\}$ is
  --- the shared-vector case behind \cref{lem:extensor-pair-in-panel}.
\end{proof}

Katoh--Tanigawa realize the base of their induction directly, at one
and two bodies (\cref{lem:theorem-55-base-producer}). Asking the hinges
at a body to pass through one point has content only at degree three or
more, so on two bodies it has content only when three or more edges
join them. The parallel-pair realization below serves every such class:
each further edge reuses one of its two hinges, and both pass through a
common point. \cref{lem:pencil-base-case} assembles this with the
edgeless and single-edge cases into the base case of
\cref{thm:pencil-reduction}.

\begin{lemma}[Two-body coincident-panel pencil realization]
  \label{lem:pencil-base-parallel-pair}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_pencilPanelRealization_parallel_pair}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-panel-realization, def:rank-hypothesis}
  Let $G$ be a two-vertex minimal-$0$-dof multigraph realizing the
  parallel-pair case (\cref{lem:theorem-55-base-producer-parallel}):
  $V(G) = \{x, y\}$ with $x \ne y$ and $E(G) = \{e, f\}$ two distinct
  edges both joining $x$ and $y$. Then $G$ has a pencil realization
  (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}) that is infinitesimally rigid on
  its two bodies $V(G) = \{x, y\}$ (\cref{def:rank-hypothesis}, the
  $V(G)$-relative rank $D$ of the $\mathrm{def}(\tilde G) = 0$ case).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:extensor-pair-through-point, lem:theorem-55-base}
  Place both bodies on a single panel $n^{\perp}$ at a fixed nonzero
  normal $n$ with a single shared concurrency point $q \in n^{\perp}$,
  and assign the two parallel hinges the two independent pencil lines
  through $q$ of \cref{lem:extensor-pair-through-point}; every edge label
  off $\{e, f\}$ reuses the first hinge, so all supporting extensors are
  nonzero. Each hinge lies in the common panel and passes through the
  common point, so the data is a pencil realization. The two independent
  supporting extensors give the combined hinge-row blocks full rank on
  the relative screw $S(x) - S(y)$, so the framework is infinitesimally
  rigid on $\{x, y\} = V(G)$ (\cref{lem:theorem-55-base}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{Pencil cycles: degree-two concurrency is automatic}
\label{sec:pencil-cycle}

Katoh--Tanigawa's cycle realization (\cref{lem:cycle-realization},
KT Lemma~5.4) places a cycle of bodies with each hinge the meet of two
adjacent panels. Every body has degree two, and at a body of degree at
most two the concurrency requirement costs nothing: two lines lying in a
common plane always meet in a point of that plane. The following records
this projective fact in the containment model, so that the cycle
realization --- indeed any hinge-coplanar realization
(\cref{def:coplanar-panel-realization}) restricted to its degree-two
bodies --- already admits the per-body concurrency point a pencil
realization asks for. The same fact accounts for two features of the
nondegeneracy conditions in \cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate}. A pencil
hub there is a body of
degree at least three, since only there does passing through one point
constrain the hinges. At a body of degree two, whose two hinges pass
through a common point for free, a further condition keeps them from
coinciding. \cref{thm:pencil-reduction} does not call on the two pencil
realizations of a cycle built below; they give a direct example of the
same fact, on the cycle realization Katoh--Tanigawa already use.

\begin{lemma}[Two coplanar hinges share a concurrency point]
  \label{lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_concurrency_point_of_extensorInPanel_pair}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:genuine-hinge-realization, def:extensor-through-point}
  Let $n \in K^4$ be nonzero and let $C_1, C_2 \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$
  be nonzero screw elements each lying in the panel with normal $n$
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}). Then there is a nonzero point
  $q \in n^{\perp}$ through which both pass
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Each hinge is the extensor of an independent pair, spanning a
  $2$-dimensional subspace of the panel $n^{\perp}$, which has dimension
  $3$ in $K^4$. Two $2$-dimensional subspaces of a $3$-dimensional space
  meet in dimension at least $2 + 2 - 3 = 1$ (the modular law), so their
  intersection contains a nonzero point $q$. Lying in each hinge's span,
  $q$ passes through both hinges; and lying in $n^{\perp}$ it is
  orthogonal to $n$.
\end{proof}

The range $3 \le m \le 4$ is exactly where the shared-normal family
exists at $d = 3$ (\cref{lem:cycle-normals}), so the triangle and the
quadrilateral are both realized. Since every body of a cycle has degree
two, the concurrency fact above (\cref{lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent})
supplies each body's point directly, alongside the shared-normal
family's panel data, giving a pencil realization outright.

\begin{lemma}[Pencil realization of a cycle]
  \label{lem:cycle-pencil-realization}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_pencilPanelRealization_cycle}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cycle-data, def:pencil-panel-realization, def:rank-hypothesis}
  Let $G$ be presented as a cycle of $m$ bodies (\cref{def:cycle-data})
  with $3 \le m \le 4$. Then $G$ has a pencil realization
  (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}) that is infinitesimally rigid on
  all of its bodies $V(G)$ (\cref{def:rank-hypothesis}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:cycle-normals, lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent, lem:cycle-realization-rigid,
        def:panel-support-extensor, def:genuine-hinge-realization, def:extensor-through-point}
  At grade $k = 2$ the cyclic shared-normal family
  (\cref{lem:cycle-normals}) supplies $m$ panel normals $n_0, \dots,
  n_{m-1}$ --- the range $3 \le m \le 4 = k + 2$ is exactly its
  hypothesis --- with each consecutive pair $n_i, n_{i+1}$ linearly
  independent. The $i$-th cycle edge is assigned the panel meet
  $C(n_i, n_{i+1})$ (\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}), nonzero and lying
  in both endpoint panels $n_i^{\perp}$, $n_{i+1}^{\perp}$
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}); every edge label off the cycle
  is given a fixed nonzero fallback, so all supporting extensors are
  nonzero. Body $i$ has exactly two incident hinges, $C(n_{i-1}, n_i)$ and
  $C(n_i, n_{i+1})$, both lying in the panel $n_i^{\perp}$; by
  \cref{lem:coplanar-hinges-concurrent} they share a nonzero point
  $q_i \in n_i^{\perp}$, through which both pass
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}). Assign body $i$ the point $q_i$.
  Then each point is nonzero and incident to its own panel, and the
  edge $C(n_i, n_{i+1})$ passes through the points of both its endpoints:
  through $q_i$ as body $i$'s second hinge, and through $q_{i+1}$ as body
  $i+1$'s first hinge (its first incident hinge is $C(n_i, n_{i+1})$
  since $(i+1) - 1 = i$). The $m$ cyclic meets are linearly independent
  (\cref{lem:cycle-normals}), so the framework is infinitesimally rigid on
  the cycle's bodies, which are all of $V(G)$ (\cref{lem:cycle-realization-rigid}).
\end{proof}

The coplanar realization below is its corollary, discarding the
per-body concurrency point: a hinge-coplanar panel realization needs
only the panel data, which the pencil realization above already
supplies, built directly from the cyclic family of shared panel
normals rather than through the meet model of the original cycle
realization.

\begin{lemma}[Coplanar realization of a cycle]
  \label{lem:cycle-coplanar-realization}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_coplanarPanelRealization_cycle}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cycle-data, def:coplanar-panel-realization, def:rank-hypothesis}
  Let $G$ be presented as a cycle of $m$ bodies (\cref{def:cycle-data})
  with $3 \le m \le 4$. Then $G$ has a hinge-coplanar panel realization
  (\cref{def:coplanar-panel-realization}) that is infinitesimally rigid on
  all of its bodies $V(G)$ (\cref{def:rank-hypothesis}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:cycle-pencil-realization}
  Immediate corollary of the pencil realization of the cycle
  (\cref{lem:cycle-pencil-realization}), discarding the per-body concurrency point: a pencil
  realization is in particular hinge-coplanar, and rigidity is unaffected by dropping a conjunct.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The two-pencil extension step}
\label{sec:pencil-extension}

Katoh--Tanigawa's induction extends a hinge-coplanar realization across
a re-added edge by supplying a supporting extensor in the meet of the
two endpoint panels (p.~670): for any two normals the common perp
$n_u^{\perp} \cap n_v^{\perp} \subseteq K^4$ has dimension at least
$4 - 2 = 2$, so it contains a nonzero hinge lying in both panels, with
no transversality of the normals needed. On the pencil condition the
re-added hinge must additionally pass through the concurrency point of
each endpoint. Where in the coplanar model a common hinge always
exists, the pencil model asks for one through two prescribed points as
well, and this constrains the two bodies: a hinge lying in both panels
and passing through both points confines each point to the common perp,
so each concurrency point must lie not only in its own panel but in the
other body's panel too. The following existence step assumes exactly
these two \emph{cross-incidences}.

\begin{lemma}[Two-pencil extension hinge]
  \label{lem:two-pencil-extension}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_extensor_two_pencils}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:genuine-hinge-realization, def:extensor-through-point}
  Let $n_u, n_v \in K^4$ be panel normals and $q_u, q_v \in K^4$
  concurrency points with $q_u$ incident to its own panel
  ($q_u \cdot n_u = 0$) and $q_v$ to its own ($q_v \cdot n_v = 0$),
  and suppose $q_u \ne 0$. If the two cross-incidences
  $q_u \cdot n_v = 0$ and $q_v \cdot n_u = 0$ hold --- each
  concurrency point lies in the other body's panel --- then there is a
  nonzero $C \in \bigwedge^{2} K^{4}$ lying in both panels
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}) and passing through both points
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Under the four incidences both points lie in the common perp
  $W = n_u^{\perp} \cap n_v^{\perp}$, which has dimension at least $2$
  in $K^4$ regardless of whether the normals are independent. It
  suffices to exhibit a $2$-dimensional subspace of $W$ containing both
  points; its extensor is then a nonzero hinge in both panels through
  both points. If $q_u$ and $q_v$ are linearly independent their span
  is such a subspace. Otherwise $q_v$ lies on the line through $q_u$,
  and since $W$ has dimension at least $2$ while that line has dimension
  $1$, $W$ contains a vector $w$ independent from $q_u$; the span of
  $q_u$ and $w$ is a $2$-dimensional subspace of $W$ containing $q_u$,
  hence also $q_v$.
\end{proof}

The two cross-incidences are not only sufficient but necessary, so the
extension hinge exists precisely when the two bodies' concurrency points
are mutually panel-incident.

\begin{lemma}[Two-pencil extension biconditional]
  \label{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_extensor_two_pencils_iff,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.dotProduct_eq_zero_of_extensorInPanel_of_extensorThroughPoint}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:two-pencil-extension, def:genuine-hinge-realization,
        def:extensor-through-point}
  With the hypotheses of \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension} --- the points
  $q_u, q_v$ incident to their own panels and $q_u \ne 0$ --- the two
  cross-incidences $q_u \cdot n_v = 0$ and $q_v \cdot n_u = 0$ hold if and
  only if there is a nonzero screw element lying in both panels
  (\cref{def:genuine-hinge-realization}) and passing through both points
  (\cref{def:extensor-through-point}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:two-pencil-extension, lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}
  The forward implication is \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension}. For the
  converse, suppose such a nonzero hinge $C$ exists. Two point pairs with the same nonzero
  $2$-extensor span the same plane (\cref{lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}), the converse of
  the point-pair proportionality of \cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union}.
  So the points through which $C$ passes and the points spanning it inside a
  panel span one and the same plane; since $C$ passes through $q_u$ and lies
  in the panel of $n_v$, the point $q_u$ lies in a span of vectors orthogonal
  to $n_v$, giving $q_u \cdot n_v = 0$, and symmetrically $q_v \cdot n_u = 0$.
\end{proof}

These cross-incidences are the chapter's first condition that the
coplanar model's extension step does not need, and three later
arguments turn on them. Katoh--Tanigawa reach a graph that is not
minimal by adding edges to a realization of a minimal spanning subgraph.
For pencil realizations the cross-incidences of the added edges cannot
always be met, so we run the induction on every multigraph
(\cref{sec:pencil-reduction}). The cut-edge case meets the
cross-incidences of its crossing edge by first repositioning one side
along a projective automorphism (\cref{lem:pencil-cut-nondegeneracy}).
At $K_4$ the necessity direction
of \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff} forces every vertex's panel
normal into a single hyperplane, so no simple realization of $K_4$ can
give all four vertices independent panel normals
(\cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate}).

\subsection{Reduction on all spanning multigraphs}
\label{sec:pencil-reduction}

This subsection states the induction behind \cref{thm:pencil-conjecture}
and proves three of its five cases. Katoh--Tanigawa's induction runs over
minimal graphs, those from which no edge can be deleted without changing
the deficiency (\cref{thm:minimal-kdof-reduction-all-k}). They reach any
other graph by realizing a minimal spanning subgraph with the same
deficiency and adding the remaining edges, each with a hinge in the meet
of its two panels (\cref{thm:theorem-55-6-multigraph}). The added edges
need not raise the rank, since the subgraph already attains the
deficiency rank.

For pencil realizations this step fails, and the failure lies in the
smaller graph. An added edge needs its two cross-incidences
(\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}), and imposing them can lower the
rank of the smaller graph. Take $K_4$ and a $4$-cycle in it, both of
deficiency $0$. A pencil realization of the $4$-cycle has rank $14$ plus
the dimension of the span of its four hinges, so it attains its
deficiency rank $18$ only when the hinges are linearly independent. The cross-incidences
of the two diagonals, with those the cycle edges satisfy already, put all
four points in every panel, so the points span at most a plane. If they
span a plane, every panel is that plane, and the hinges lie in the
three-dimensional space of its lines. If they span a line, at least two
edges of the cycle join bodies with different points, and both of their
hinges are that line. If they coincide, all four hinges pass through one
point. In each case the hinges span at most three dimensions, so no
pencil realization of the $4$-cycle at its deficiency rank admits the
diagonals.

We therefore run the induction on every multigraph. Its case analysis
still needs a vertex of degree two when there is no proper rigid
subgraph. Katoh--Tanigawa find one by an edge count that uses minimality
(KT Lemmas~4.5 and~4.6, pp.~663--664). Here a counting fact takes its
place: a loopless multigraph whose vertices all have degree at least
three has a proper rigid subgraph (\cref{lem:pencil-min-degree-rigid}).
So a graph without one has a vertex of degree at most two, and
two-edge-connectivity forces that degree to be exactly two. The
statement the induction proves is the following.

\begin{definition}[Pencil realization at the deficiency rank]
  \label{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.HasPencilRealization}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-panel-realization, def:D-deficiency}
  A \emph{pencil realization at the deficiency rank} of a multigraph $G$ is
  a pencil realization $(F, n, q)$ of $G$
  (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}) whose rigidity rows span dimension
  \[
    \dim \operatorname{span}(\text{rigidity rows of } F)
      = D(|V(G)| - 1) - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G),
  \]
  the target of \cref{thm:theorem-55-6-multigraph} restricted to the pencil
  condition.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Proper rigid subgraph from a minimum degree of three]
  \label{lem:pencil-min-degree-rigid}
  \lean{Graph.exists_isProperRigidSubgraph_of_three_le_degree}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:rigid-subgraph, def:matroid-MG}
  Let $G$ be a loopless multigraph with $|V(G)| \ge 3$ and $D = \binom{d+1}{2}
  \ge 4$. If every vertex of $G$ has degree at least three, $G$ has a proper
  rigid subgraph.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Let $v$ be a vertex of minimum degree $\delta \ge 3$. The handshake
  identity gives $\delta\,|V(G)| \le 2\,|E(G)|$, so the edges avoiding $v$
  number $|E(G)| - \delta \ge \delta(|V(G)|-2)/2$, and the $(D-1)$-fold
  fiber of these edges in $M(\tilde G)$ has size $(D-1)(|E(G)| - \delta) >
  D(|V(G)| - 2)$ once $D \ge 4$ --- strictly more than the $(D,D)$-sparsity
  bound on any vertex set omitting $v$. The fiber is therefore dependent in
  $M(\tilde G)$ and contains a circuit, whose induced subgraph is rigid
  (\cref{lem:circuit-induces-rigid}), spans at least two vertices, and
  omits $v$, hence is proper.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Reduction on all spanning multigraphs; cf.\ KT Theorem 4.9]
  \label{thm:pencil-reduction}
  \lean{Graph.pencil_reduction}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cut-edges-2ec, def:rigid-subgraph, lem:pencil-min-degree-rigid}
  A property $P$ of multigraphs that is closed under five cases holds of
  every nonempty multigraph. The five cases are: \textup{(i)} $G$ has a
  loop (delete it and reinsert it); \textup{(ii)} $G$ is loopless with
  $|V(G)| \le 2$ (base case); \textup{(iii)} $G$ is loopless, $|V(G)| \ge
  3$, and not two-edge-connected (cut-edge decomposition,
  \cref{def:cut-edges-2ec}); \textup{(iv)} $G$ is loopless, $|V(G)| \ge 3$,
  and has a proper rigid subgraph (\cref{def:rigid-subgraph}); \textup{(v)}
  $G$ is loopless, two-edge-connected, $|V(G)| \ge 3$, has no proper rigid
  subgraph, and (by \cref{lem:pencil-min-degree-rigid}) some vertex has
  degree exactly two. Case (i) is handed the property at every nonempty
  multigraph smaller than $G$ in the lexicographic order on
  $(|V(G)|, |E(G)|)$. Cases (iii), (iv) and (v) are handed it at every
  nonempty multigraph on fewer vertices than $G$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Strong induction on $(|V(G)|, |E(G)|)$, lexicographically. Its
  hypothesis at $G$ gives the property at every smaller nonempty
  multigraph, in particular at every one on fewer vertices. Deleting a
  loop keeps $|V(G)|$ and lowers $|E(G)|$, so it gives a smaller
  multigraph. Case (v)'s degree-two vertex follows from
  \cref{lem:pencil-min-degree-rigid} (no proper rigid subgraph forces a
  vertex of degree at most two) and two-edge-connectivity (degree at least
  two).
\end{proof}

We take the five cases in the order of the lemmas below. In case~(v)
the graph is simple (\cref{lem:pencil-simple-of-noRigid}). The loop
case~(i) is \cref{lem:pencil-loop-case}. The cut-edge case~(iii) is
\cref{lem:pencil-cut-case}, after two lemmas on projective
automorphisms. The base case~(ii) is \cref{lem:pencil-base-case}. The
contraction and split cases, (iv) and~(v), are left for later
subsections. \Cref{sec:pencil-nondegenerate} strengthens the statement
that the induction proves. \Cref{sec:pencil-main-component-route} then
proves both cases by one argument, which rests on
\cref{sec:main-component}. That argument and \cref{sec:main-component}
contract proper rigid subgraphs, using
\cref{lem:pencil-contraction-deficiency}: contraction leaves the
deficiency unchanged.

\begin{lemma}[Simplicity from looplessness and no proper rigid subgraph]
  \label{lem:pencil-simple-of-noRigid}
  \lean{Graph.simple_of_loopless_of_noRigid}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:rigid-subgraph}
  Let $D \ge 2$, and let $G$ be a loopless multigraph with $|V(G)| \ge 3$
  and no proper rigid subgraph (\cref{def:rigid-subgraph}). Then $G$ is
  simple.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Suppose two edges join vertices $x$ and $y$. Their $(D-1)$-fold copies
  are $2(D-1) \ge D$ parallel edges on $\{x, y\}$, hence $D$
  edge-disjoint spanning trees, so the subgraph induced on $\{x, y\}$ is
  rigid, as in the proof of KT Lemma~6.2 (p.~673). It is proper, as
  $|V(G)| \ge 3$, a contradiction. So $G$ has no parallel edges, and
  being loopless it is simple.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The loop case]
  \label{lem:pencil-loop-case}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasPencilRealization_of_isLoopAt}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis, lem:two-pencil-extension}
  Let $e$ be a loop of $G$ at a vertex $v$. If $G - e$ has a pencil
  realization at the deficiency rank (\cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}),
  then so does $G$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Keep the realization of $G - e$, and give $e$ a nonzero hinge that lies
  in the panel of $v$ and passes through the point of $v$. It exists by
  \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension} with $u = v$, where both
  cross-incidences become the incidence of the point of $v$ with its own
  panel. The loop adds only zero rows, since both of its ends are $v$. A
  loop crosses no partition of $V(G)$, so deleting it leaves the
  deficiency unchanged, and the rank is still the deficiency rank.
\end{proof}

The cut-edge case moves one side of a realization by a projective
automorphism. The next lemma shows that such a move keeps a pencil
realization at the deficiency rank, and the one after it chooses the
automorphism.

\begin{lemma}[Projective transport of a pencil realization]
  \label{lem:pencil-projective-transport}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasPencilPanelRealization_mapExtensor_screwEquivOfLinearEquiv,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensorInPanel_screwEquivOfLinearEquiv,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensorThroughPoint_screwEquivOfLinearEquiv}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-panel-realization}
  Let $g$ be a linear automorphism of $K^4$ and $h$ a contragredient of it,
  meaning $\langle g x, h y\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle$ for the standard
  pairing. Transporting a pencil panel realization $(F, n, q)$ by acting on
  hinges through the induced automorphism of the screw space, on the
  concurrency points $q$ by $g$, and on the panel normals $n$ by $h$, again
  gives a pencil panel realization on the same multigraph. Its rigidity-row
  span has the same dimension, so a pencil realization at the deficiency
  rank transports to one.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:screw-map-rows}
  Panel containment and through-point incidence transport because the
  induced screw automorphism sends the extensor of spanning points
  $p_0, \dots$ to the extensor of $g p_0, \dots$; the contragredient
  identity turns each incidence $\langle p_i, n\rangle = 0$ into $\langle g
  p_i, h n\rangle = 0$, and $g$ sends the span containing $q$ to
  the one containing $g q$. The panel--point incidence $\langle q,
  n\rangle = 0$ is likewise preserved, and the automorphisms send nonzero
  data to nonzero data. Rank invariance is the change-of-screw-coordinates
  identity (\cref{lem:screw-map-rows}): this lemma is the collineation
  companion of \cref{lem:pencil-self-dual}, whose polarity is instead a
  correlation, exchanging the normal and point roles rather than
  repositioning within them; both rest on the same field-general
  screw-space automorphism mechanism (\cref{thm:projective-invariance}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Existence of a repositioning automorphism]
  \label{lem:pencil-cut-nondegeneracy}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_reposition_cross_incidences,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_contragredient_linearEquiv,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exists_perp_linearIndependent}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}
  Fix the panel/point data of the two sides at a crossing edge $uv$: normal
  $n_1(u)$ and point $pt_1(u) \ne 0$ on the $V_1$ side, normal $n_2(v)$ and
  point $pt_2(v) \ne 0$ on the $V_2$ side. Then there is a linear
  automorphism $g$ of $K^4$ with a contragredient $h$ ($\langle g x, h
  y\rangle = \langle x, y\rangle$ for the standard pairing) meeting the two
  cross-incidences of \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff} once the $V_2$
  side is transported by $(g, h)$: $\langle pt_1(u), h\, n_2(v)\rangle = 0$
  and $\langle g\, pt_2(v), n_1(u)\rangle = 0$; moreover the transported
  point $g\, pt_2(v)$ is projectively distinct from $pt_1(u)$, which the
  construction supplies for free.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Explicit, over any field. Every automorphism of $K^4$ has a
  contragredient, the inverse-transpose. Pick $a \in n_1(u)^\perp$ with
  $\{a, pt_1(u)\}$ independent and $b \in n_2(v)^\perp$ with $\{b,
  pt_2(v)\}$ independent, possible because each hyperplane $\perp$ is at
  least $3$-dimensional and so is not contained in a line. Let $g$ be the
  frame automorphism sending $pt_2(v) \mapsto a$ and $b \mapsto pt_1(u)$.
  Then $g\, pt_2(v) = a \in n_1(u)^\perp$ is the second incidence, and $g\,
  b = pt_1(u)$ with the contragredient identity turns the first into $b \in
  n_2(v)^\perp$. The distinctness clause is the independence of $\{a,
  pt_1(u)\}$ chosen at the start, read through $g\, pt_2(v) = a$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The cut-edge case]
  \label{lem:pencil-cut-case}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasPencilRealization_of_not_twoEdgeConnected,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasPencilRealization_of_not_twoEdgeConnected_core,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasDistinctPencilRealization_of_not_twoEdgeConnected}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis, def:cut-edges-2ec,
        def:pencil-distinct-motive,
        lem:two-pencil-extension-iff, lem:pencil-projective-transport,
        lem:pencil-cut-nondegeneracy}
  Let $G$ be a multigraph that is not two-edge-connected, so that at most
  one edge crosses some nonempty proper subset $V_1 \subsetneq V(G)$, and
  let $V_2 = V(G) \setminus V_1$. If $G[V_1]$ and $G[V_2]$ each have a
  pencil realization at the deficiency rank, then so does $G$. The same
  holds for realizations at the deficiency rank in which the two points of
  every link are projectively distinct.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:pencil-projective-transport, lem:pencil-cut-nondegeneracy,
        lem:cut-edge-decomposition, lem:two-pencil-extension}
  Realize the two sides separately. If no edge crosses, together they
  realize $G$. Suppose one edge $uv$ crosses, with $u \in V_1$. The two
  realizations were chosen independently, so the cross-incidences of $uv$
  need not hold, and without them $uv$ has no hinge
  (\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}). So we first move the side $V_2$ by
  the projective automorphism of \cref{lem:pencil-cut-nondegeneracy}, after
  which both cross-incidences hold. The moved side is still a pencil
  realization at the deficiency rank
  (\cref{lem:pencil-projective-transport}). Now
  \cref{lem:two-pencil-extension} gives $uv$ a hinge. In both cases the
  ranks of the parts add up to the deficiency rank of $G$, as in
  Katoh--Tanigawa's cut-edge case (\cref{lem:case-cut-edge-realization}). For the
  second form, the points along a link inside a side stay distinct under
  the automorphism, and at $uv$ they are distinct by the last clause of
  \cref{lem:pencil-cut-nondegeneracy}.
\end{proof}
\begin{fmlnote}
  \label{fmlnote:pencil-cut-case}
  The two forms are one Lean theorem whose parameter selects the form;
  the pinned names are that theorem and its two specializations.
\end{fmlnote}

\begin{lemma}[The base case, $|V(G)| \le 2$]
  \label{lem:pencil-base-case}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasPencilRealization_of_ncard_le_two}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis, lem:pencil-base-parallel-pair}
  Every loopless multigraph with at most two vertices has a pencil
  realization at the deficiency rank (\cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Give every body the same normal $n$ and the same point $q$ of its
  panel. With no edge the deficiency rank is $0$. With one edge it is
  $D - 1$, which one nonzero hinge in the panel through $q$ attains.
  With a class of two or more parallel edges it is $D$. Two edges of the
  class get the two independent hinges of
  \cref{lem:pencil-base-parallel-pair}, which make the two bodies rigid,
  and every further edge reuses the second hinge.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Deficiency is unchanged by contracting a proper rigid subgraph]
  \label{lem:pencil-contraction-deficiency}
  \lean{Graph.rigidContract_deficiency_eq}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:rigid-subgraph, lem:contract-deficiency-conservation}
  Let $H$ be a proper rigid subgraph of $G$. Contracting $H$ to a single
  vertex leaves the deficiency unchanged,
  $\operatorname{def}(\widetilde{G / E(H)}) = \operatorname{def}(\tilde
  G)$, without assuming $G$ minimal.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The matroid-contraction rank identity and the rank/deficiency bridge
  behind \cref{lem:contract-deficiency-conservation} already use no
  minimality; only the base/fiber-meeting half of the corresponding
  minimality-tracking statement did, and that half is not needed here.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Nondegenerate realizations and the conditioned pair}
\label{sec:pencil-nondegenerate}

Katoh and Tanigawa's induction statement asks for more than the rank.
When the graph is simple, their realization must also be nonparallel,
that is, no two of its panels are parallel (KT p.~668; KT Theorem~5.5,
p.~670). This subsection defines a pencil analogue, the nondegenerate
pencil realization, and strengthens the statement of the induction to
include it. The strengthened statement, the conditioned pair, asks for a
nondegenerate realization at the deficiency rank under two conditions on
the graph. We show that neither condition can be dropped. The
subsection ends with the reduction of \cref{thm:pencil-reduction} for the
conditioned pair. Throughout, $k = 2$, so panel normals and concurrency
points lie in $K^4$.

The pencil condition constrains the hinges at a body only when the body
has three or more of them (\cref{sec:pencil-realization}). The next
definition calls such a body a hub. It asks for independence among the
normals and among the points around each body.

\begin{definition}[Nondegenerate pencil realization, feasibility]
  \label{def:pencil-nondegenerate}
  \lean{Graph.PencilHub, Graph.closedHubNbhd, Graph.closedNbhd,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.IsNondegPencilRealization,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.PencilNondegFeasible}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-panel-realization}
  A vertex of a multigraph is a \emph{pencil hub} when it has degree at
  least three. The \emph{closed hub-neighbourhood} of a vertex $v$ is the
  set of pencil hubs among $v$ itself and its neighbours, and the
  \emph{closed neighbourhood} of $v$ is $v$ together with all of its
  neighbours, hub or not. A pencil realization of $G$
  (\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}) is \emph{nondegenerate} when the
  two concurrency points of every link are projectively distinct; at every
  vertex, the panel normals of its closed hub-neighbourhood are linearly
  independent; and at every non-hub vertex, the concurrency points of its
  closed neighbourhood are linearly independent. A multigraph is
  \emph{nondegeneracy-feasible} when it has a nondegenerate pencil
  realization.
\end{definition}

The first condition makes the hinge of every link the line through the
points of its two ends (\cref{lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}). The
other two are limited by the incidences of a pencil realization. The hinge
of an edge $vw$ is nonzero, lies in the panel of $w$ and passes through
$q_v$, so $q_v \cdot n_w = 0$ (\cref{lem:two-pencil-extension-iff}).
Thus the normals of $v$ and its neighbours lie in the three-dimensional
space $q_v^{\perp}$, and their points lie in the panel $n_v^{\perp}$,
also of dimension three. Hence the normals of a closed
hub-neighbourhood are independent only if it has at most three members. At $K_4$ every body is
a hub and every closed hub-neighbourhood has four members, so $K_4$ is not
nondegeneracy-feasible. Likewise, the points of a closed neighbourhood are
independent only if it has at most three members, which rules out every
hub of a simple graph. The third condition is asked only at bodies that
are not hubs. At such a body it follows from the first condition and
$q_v \neq 0$, unless the body has two distinct neighbours. There it asks
that the two hinges at the body be distinct lines. The first two
conditions do not imply this. The path on three bodies has a pencil
realization whose three points are distinct but collinear, and both of its
hinges are then the same line.

In the next definition, generic names only the property stated, not an
algebraic independence of coordinates.

\begin{definition}[Generic pencil realization]
  \label{def:pencil-generic-motive}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.HasGenericPencilRealization}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-nondegenerate, def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}
  A multigraph $G$ \emph{has a generic pencil realization} when it has a
  nondegenerate pencil realization (\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate})
  whose rigidity rows attain the deficiency-rank target of
  \cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}.
\end{definition}

Keeping only the first condition of \cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate} gives
a weaker notion, which every simple multigraph with at least one body
satisfies at the deficiency rank (\cref{thm:pencil-conjecture}). We do not
add this condition to \cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}, for two reasons.
It would break the symmetry noted after \cref{lem:pencil-self-dual}. Also,
some multigraphs would have no pencil realization at the deficiency rank.
Two bodies joined by two edges have deficiency rank $D = 6$, which needs
two independent hinges. At distinct points both hinges are the line
through the two points, and the rank is $5$.

\begin{definition}[Adjacent-distinct pencil realization]
  \label{def:pencil-distinct-motive}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.HasDistinctPencilRealization}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis, def:pencil-nondegenerate}
  A multigraph $G$ \emph{has an adjacent-distinct pencil realization}
  when it has a pencil realization at the deficiency rank
  (\cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}) in which the two concurrency points
  of every link are projectively distinct.
\end{definition}

A generic pencil realization is adjacent-distinct, and an
adjacent-distinct one is in particular a pencil realization at the
deficiency rank.

The induction statement asks for a generic realization under two
conditions. Simplicity alone, the condition of KT Theorem~5.5, is not
enough, since $K_4$ is simple and not nondegeneracy-feasible.
Nondegeneracy-feasibility alone is not enough either. Two bodies joined
by two edges are nondegeneracy-feasible. Neither is a hub, so it suffices
to take two distinct panels, two distinct points on their common line, and
that line as both hinges. By the count above, none of their nondegenerate
realizations attains the deficiency rank. So the statement asks for both
conditions.

\begin{definition}[The conditioned pair]
  \label{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.PencilPair}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-nondegenerate, def:pencil-generic-motive,
        def:pencil-distinct-motive, def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}
  A multigraph $G$ satisfies the \emph{conditioned pair} at $n$ when it
  has a pencil realization at the deficiency rank
  (\cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}); when, if it is simple, it has an
  adjacent-distinct one (\cref{def:pencil-distinct-motive}); and when, if
  it is both simple and nondegeneracy-feasible
  (\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}), it has a generic one
  (\cref{def:pencil-generic-motive}).
\end{definition}
\begin{fmlnote}
  \label{fmlnote:pencil-conditioned-pair}
  In the Lean, this definition and \cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis},
  \cref{def:pencil-generic-motive} and \cref{def:pencil-distinct-motive}
  take the parameter $n$ of the deficiency (\cref{def:D-deficiency}, with
  $D = \binom{n+1}{2}$) as an argument, while their realizations stay at
  $k = 2$, where the target has $D = 6$. The theorems that use them take
  $n = 3$, where the two agree.
\end{fmlnote}

We now prove the reduction for the conditioned pair, beginning with the
three cases that \cref{sec:pencil-reduction} proves for the bare
statement.

\begin{lemma}[The conditioned pair at a loop, on at most two bodies and at a cut edge]
  \label{lem:pencil-pair-loop-base-cut}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.pencilPair_loop_base_cut}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-conditioned-pair, def:cut-edges-2ec}
  Let $K$ be an infinite field and $G$ a multigraph.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If $G$ has a loop, and every nonempty multigraph with fewer
      vertices than $G$, or with as many vertices and fewer edges,
      satisfies the conditioned pair at $n = 3$
      (\cref{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}), then so does $G$.
    \item If $G$ is loopless and has one or two vertices, it satisfies
      the conditioned pair at $n = 3$.
    \item If $G$ is loopless with $|V(G)| \ge 3$ and not
      two-edge-connected (\cref{def:cut-edges-2ec}), and every nonempty
      multigraph on fewer vertices than $G$ satisfies the conditioned
      pair at $n = 3$, then so does $G$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:pencil-loop-case, lem:pencil-base-case, lem:pencil-cut-case,
        def:pencil-nondegenerate}
  In each case the bare conjunct is \cref{lem:pencil-loop-case},
  \cref{lem:pencil-base-case} or \cref{lem:pencil-cut-case}. In case~(1)
  the first of these applies to $G$ with the loop deleted, which has as
  many vertices as $G$ and fewer edges. The other two conjuncts are
  needed only when $G$ is simple. A multigraph with a loop is not simple.
  A simple multigraph on at most two bodies has at most one edge, and a
  direct construction gives it a nondegenerate realization at the
  deficiency rank. At a cut edge, the adjacent-distinct conjunct is the
  second form of \cref{lem:pencil-cut-case}.

  It remains to prove the generic conjunct in case~(3). Let $G$ be simple
  and nondegeneracy-feasible, with at most one edge crossing a nonempty
  proper subset $V_1$ of $V(G)$. A nondegenerate realization of $G$
  restricts to one of a subgraph, provided no body of the subgraph drops
  from degree three or more to degree two. So, except in the last case
  below, each smaller graph used is nondegeneracy-feasible, and the
  hypothesis of case~(3) gives it a generic realization. If no edge
  crosses, the generic realizations of the two sides together form one of
  $G$. Suppose one edge $uv$ crosses, with $u \in V_1$. If both sides have
  at least two bodies, we use each side together with the far end of
  $uv$, so that $u$ keeps its degree in the first and $v$ in the second.
  We move the second as in \cref{lem:pencil-cut-case}, by an automorphism
  that also avoids finitely many subspaces, and join the two along $uv$.

  If one side is a single body, say $v$, we use $G - v$, and give $v$ a
  point and a panel that avoid finitely many subspaces. When $u$ has
  degree three, it drops to degree two in $G - v$. Then we first show
  that $G - v$ is nondegeneracy-feasible, and move its generic
  realization, at the same rank, to one that also meets the conditions of
  $G$ on the normals at $u$ and its other two neighbours. Both steps
  choose a point off the zero set of a nonzero polynomial, which exists
  because $K$ is infinite.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The conditioned pair from the contraction and split cases]
  \label{thm:pencil-conditional-realization-pair}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.pencil_conjecture_of_arms_pair}
  \leanok
  \uses{thm:pencil-reduction, def:pencil-conditioned-pair, def:rigid-subgraph,
        def:cut-edges-2ec}
  Let $K$ be an infinite field. Consider two kinds of loopless
  multigraphs $G$ with $|V(G)| \ge 3$: those with a proper rigid subgraph
  (\cref{def:rigid-subgraph}), and those that are two-edge-connected,
  have no proper rigid subgraph and have a vertex of degree two. Suppose
  that each such $G$ satisfies the conditioned pair at $n = 3$
  (\cref{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}) whenever every nonempty multigraph
  on fewer vertices than $G$ does. Then every nonempty multigraph
  satisfies the conditioned pair at $n = 3$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{thm:pencil-reduction, lem:pencil-pair-loop-base-cut}
  Apply \cref{thm:pencil-reduction} with the conditioned pair at $n = 3$
  as the property. Its cases at a loop, on at most two vertices and at a
  cut edge are \cref{lem:pencil-pair-loop-base-cut}. Its contraction and
  split cases are the two hypotheses.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reduction to the main component}
\label{sec:pencil-main-component-route}

This subsection proves the two hypotheses of
\cref{thm:pencil-conditional-realization-pair}, the contraction and
split cases for the conditioned pair, and with them the pencil
conjecture. At a simple two-edge-connected multigraph, both cases follow
from two statements: the multigraph has an adjacent-distinct pencil
realization at the deficiency rank, and a generic one when it is
nondegeneracy-feasible. These are the
main-component statements, proved in \cref{sec:main-component}. A
loopless two-edge-connected multigraph that is not simple has two
parallel edges, and we contract them, as Katoh and Tanigawa do
(KT Lemma~6.2).

The idea is to build pencil realizations by lifting planar pictures.
Place each body $v$ at a point $q_v = (x_v, y_v)$ of the plane, with
adjacent bodies at distinct points and no closed neighbourhood on a
line. Such a picture is admissible (\cref{def:pencil-admissible-picture}).
Lift $v$ to the point $p_v = (x_v, y_v, z_v, 1)$ of $K^4$ at a height
$z_v$ (\cref{def:pencil-configuration}). Take $p_v$ as the concurrency
point of $v$, and the line through $p_u$ and $p_v$ as the hinge of an
edge $uv$. Every hinge then passes through the points of its two ends.
The hinges at $v$ lie in a common panel exactly when the points of the
closed neighbourhood of $v$ lie in a plane. So the lifted configuration
is a pencil realization when every closed neighbourhood is coplanar.
Over a fixed picture $q$ this is a linear condition on the heights
(\cref{lem:pencil-condition-linear}), and the heights that satisfy it
form a linear space $L(q)$, the lifting space
(\cref{def:pencil-lifting-space}).

Over the admissible pictures at which $\dim L(q)$ is least, these
configurations form a vector bundle. Its closure is the main component
of the configuration space (\cref{rem:pencil-x0-main-component}). In
\cref{sec:main-component} we show that, at every simple connected
multigraph whose bodies all have degree at least two, a general
configuration of the main component has the deficiency rank
(\cref{thm:pencil-x0-generic-attains}). The proof is a strong induction
on the number of bodies. A lifted configuration at the deficiency rank
is an adjacent-distinct pencil realization
(\cref{lem:pencil-config-distinct-realization}), which gives the first
statement. The second does not follow in this way. When two pencil hubs
have three common neighbours, as in $K_{2,3}$, no configuration over a
general picture is nondegenerate
(\cref{lem:pencil-x0-two-hubs-obstruction}). We prove the second
statement instead inside the induction of \cref{thm:pencil-reduction},
from the conditioned pair at smaller multigraphs
(\cref{thm:pencil-generic-step}).

\begin{definition}[The two main-component statements]
  \label{def:pencil-main-component-statements}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.X0Dist,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.X0Gen}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-distinct-motive, def:pencil-generic-motive,
        def:pencil-nondegenerate}
  Let $K$ be a field. The \emph{distinct main-component statement}
  asserts that every simple two-edge-connected multigraph on at least
  three bodies has an adjacent-distinct pencil realization at the
  deficiency rank at $n = 3$ (\cref{def:pencil-distinct-motive}). The
  \emph{generic main-component statement} asserts that every such
  multigraph that is nondegeneracy-feasible
  (\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}) has a generic pencil realization at
  $n = 3$ (\cref{def:pencil-generic-motive}).
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[A multigraph with parallel edges; cf.\ KT Lemma~6.2]
  \label{lem:pencil-nonsimple-case}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.hasPencilRealization_of_not_simple}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis, def:pencil-conditioned-pair,
        def:rigid-subgraph}
  Let $K$ be a field and $G$ a loopless multigraph on at least three
  bodies that is not simple. If every multigraph on strictly fewer
  bodies satisfies the conditioned pair at $n = 3$
  (\cref{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}), then $G$ has a pencil realization
  at the deficiency rank (\cref{def:pencil-rank-hypothesis}).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:case-I-realization-nonsimple, lem:pencil-contraction-deficiency,
        lem:rigidityRows-splice-rank-add, lem:extensor-pair-through-given-point,
        lem:theorem-55-base, lem:relative-deficiency-rank-bound}
  We follow the proof of KT Lemma~6.2~\cite[p.~673]{katohTanigawa2011},
  as in \cref{lem:case-I-realization-nonsimple}, for pencil realizations
  and without minimality.

  Let $e$ and $f$ be parallel edges joining bodies $a$ and $b$. They span
  a proper rigid subgraph $H$ on $\{a, b\}$, as in the proof of
  \cref{lem:pencil-simple-of-noRigid}. Contracting $H$ removes one body
  and leaves the deficiency unchanged
  (\cref{lem:pencil-contraction-deficiency}). So by the induction
  hypothesis the contraction $G/H$ has a pencil realization at the
  deficiency rank. Give $a$ and $b$ the panel and the point of the
  contracted body, and keep every other hinge. Then realize $e$ and $f$
  by two linearly independent lines through that point, inside that
  panel. The point is fixed by the realization of the contraction, and
  two such lines exist through any point of a panel
  (\cref{lem:extensor-pair-through-given-point}). Every hinge now lies in
  the panels and passes through the points of both of its ends, so this
  is a pencil realization of $G$.

  It remains to compute the rank. The two independent hinges make
  $\{a, b\}$ infinitesimally rigid (\cref{lem:theorem-55-base}). As in
  Katoh and Tanigawa's proof (KT eqs.~(6.3)--(6.5), pp.~673--674), the
  rigidity matrix is block triangular. So the rows of $e$ and $f$ add
  $D = 6$ to the rank of the contraction's realization
  (\cref{lem:rigidityRows-splice-rank-add}), and the rank is at least
  \[
    D(|V(G/H)| - 1) - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G) + D
      \;=\; D(|V(G)| - 1) - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G).
  \]
  No pencil realization of $G$ has a larger rank
  (\cref{lem:relative-deficiency-rank-bound}), so this is the deficiency
  rank.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Pencil realization from the two main-component statements]
  \label{thm:pencil-conditional-realization-main-component}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.pencilPair_of_X0,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.pencil_conjecture_of_X0}
  \leanok
  \uses{thm:pencil-reduction, def:pencil-conditioned-pair,
        def:pencil-main-component-statements}
  Let $K$ be an infinite field. If the distinct and the generic
  main-component statements (\cref{def:pencil-main-component-statements})
  hold, then every multigraph on the whole ambient body set satisfies the
  conditioned pair at $n = 3$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{thm:pencil-conditional-realization-pair,
        lem:pencil-pair-loop-base-cut, lem:pencil-nonsimple-case}
  By \cref{thm:pencil-conditional-realization-pair}, it suffices to
  prove the contraction and split cases for the conditioned pair, and one
  argument proves both. Let $G$ be a loopless multigraph on at
  least three bodies, and suppose that every nonempty multigraph on fewer
  bodies satisfies the conditioned pair. There are three cases.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If $G$ is not two-edge-connected, it satisfies the conditioned
      pair by the cut-edge case of \cref{lem:pencil-pair-loop-base-cut}.
    \item If $G$ is two-edge-connected and simple, the two statements
      give its adjacent-distinct and generic conjuncts. An
      adjacent-distinct realization is in particular one at the
      deficiency rank, which is the bare conjunct.
    \item If $G$ is two-edge-connected and not simple, its
      adjacent-distinct and generic conjuncts are vacuous, and its bare
      conjunct follows from \cref{lem:pencil-nonsimple-case}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The pencil conjecture]
  \label{thm:pencil-conjecture}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.pencil_conjecture,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.pencilPair_of_nonempty}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}
  Let $K$ be an infinite field. Every multigraph on the whole ambient
  body set satisfies the conditioned pair at $n = 3$
  (\cref{def:pencil-conditioned-pair}): it has a pencil realization at
  the deficiency rank, with adjacent concurrency points distinct when it
  is simple, and a generic one when it is also nondegeneracy-feasible.
  More generally, so does every multigraph with at least one body.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{thm:pencil-conditional-realization-main-component,
        thm:pencil-x0-generic-attains, thm:pencil-conditioned-pair-nonempty}
  The two main-component statements hold over every infinite field
  (\cref{thm:pencil-x0-generic-attains}). So the first claim follows from
  \cref{thm:pencil-conditional-realization-main-component}. The second
  claim is \cref{thm:pencil-conditioned-pair-nonempty}, on which the
  proof of the generic statement rests.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Girth and degree-two chains under no proper rigid subgraph}
\label{sec:pencil-girth-chain}

This subsection concerns a vertex $v$ of degree two in a simple
two-edge-connected multigraph $G$ with no proper rigid subgraph
(\cref{def:rigid-subgraph}). One neighbour of $v$ is not a pencil hub
(\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}). We prove what these hypotheses imply
about the shape of $G$ around $v$. The proof of
\cref{thm:pencil-conjecture} does not use these results.

Let $D = \binom{n+1}{2} \ge 3$. In a multigraph with no proper rigid
subgraph, a cycle on at most $D$ vertices is rigid, and so it passes
through every vertex (\cref{lem:pencil-short-cycle-spanning}). Hence the
girth of $G$ exceeds $D$ once some vertex has degree at least three
(\cref{lem:pencil-girth-of-hub}). At $n = 3$, where $D = 6$, the girth is
then at least seven. So the closed neighbourhoods of two distinct
vertices share at most two vertices
(\cref{lem:pencil-closed-nbhd-girth-five}).

The neighbour of $v$ that is not a pencil hub has degree two, since $G$ is
two-edge-connected (\cref{def:cut-edges-2ec}). There are then three cases
(\cref{lem:pencil-degree-two-chain}). First, $G$ may be a cycle. Second,
$v$ may lie on a cycle through a single pencil hub whose other vertices
have degree two; that hub is then a cut vertex of $G$. Third, $v$ may lie
on a chain $w u_1 \dots u_m w'$ between two distinct pencil hubs, whose
$m \ge 2$ interior vertices have degree two.

In the third case the interior vertices have no edges off the chain, so
$\{w, w'\}$ separates them from the rest of $G$. Deleting them leaves a
connected multigraph, in which $w$ and $w'$ each have one edge fewer
(\cref{lem:pencil-chain-side-connected}). Across this separation, the
rank of a realization of $G$ is determined by its restrictions to the two
sides (\cref{cor:block-rank-vertex-two-cut}). Every path from $w$ to $w'$
that avoids the interior vertices has at least $D - m$ edges
(\cref{lem:pencil-chain-side-distance}). In particular, $w$ and $w'$ are
not adjacent when $m \le D - 2$.

The structural coverage of \cref{sec:main-component-coverage} uses chains
in the same sense, between two vertices of degree at least three, and
allows a single interior vertex (\cref{def:pencil-x0-chain}). Where the
coverage extracts chains, the graph is 2-connected and has a vertex of
degree other than two, so the first two cases cannot occur. The coverage
finds its chains directly (\cref{lem:pencil-x0-chain-exists}) and does
not use this subsection.

\begin{definition}[Girth]
  \label{def:girth}
  \lean{Graph.GirthGE, Graph.GirthGE.mono, Graph.GirthGE.anti}
  \leanok
  A \emph{cycle on $m$ vertices} of a multigraph $G$, for $m \ge 3$,
  consists of $m$ distinct vertices $v_0, \dots, v_{m-1}$ and $m$ distinct
  edges $e_0, \dots, e_{m-1}$ with $e_i$ joining $v_i$ to $v_{i+1}$, indices
  taken modulo $m$. The multigraph $G$ has \emph{girth at least $g$} when it
  has no cycle on $m$ vertices for any $3 \le m < g$. In particular, loops
  and pairs of parallel edges are not cycles.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Short cycles are spanning]
  \label{lem:pencil-short-cycle-spanning}
  \lean{Graph.range_vtx_eq_vertexSet_of_cycle_of_noRigid}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:girth, def:rigid-subgraph}
  Let $G$ be a multigraph with no proper rigid subgraph and
  $D = \binom{n+1}{2} \ge 3$. Then every cycle of $G$ on $m$ vertices with
  $3 \le m \le D$ passes through every vertex of $G$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:chain-cycle-dichotomy, def:rigid-subgraph}
  Let $H$ be the subgraph formed by the cycle's vertices and edges, and
  let $P$ be a partition of $V(H)$ into $p \ge 2$ parts. Each part contains
  a vertex whose successor along the cycle lies in another part, so at
  least $p$ edges of $H$ join distinct parts. Hence
  $\operatorname{def}_{\tilde H}(P) \le D(p - 1) - (D - 1)p = p - D \le 0$,
  as $p \le m \le D$. So $\operatorname{def}(\tilde H) = 0$, and $H$ is
  rigid, as in the proof of \cref{lem:chain-cycle-dichotomy}. It has at least two vertices, so if its
  vertex set were a proper subset of $V(G)$ it would be a proper rigid
  subgraph.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Girth exceeds $D$ once some vertex has degree three]
  \label{lem:pencil-girth-of-hub}
  \lean{Graph.girthGE_of_noRigid_of_three_le_degree}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:girth, def:rigid-subgraph}
  Let $G$ be a simple multigraph with no proper rigid subgraph,
  $D = \binom{n+1}{2} \ge 3$, and suppose some vertex of $G$ has degree at
  least three. Then $G$ has girth at least $D + 1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:pencil-short-cycle-spanning}
  Suppose $G$ has a cycle $C$ on $m \le D$ vertices. By
  \cref{lem:pencil-short-cycle-spanning} it passes through every vertex, so
  $|V(G)| = m$ and the vertex $w$ of degree at least three lies on $C$.
  Let $g$ be an edge at $w$ other than its two edges on $C$. As $G$ has no
  loop, $g$ joins $w$ to another vertex $z$ of $C$. As $G$ has no parallel
  edges, $z$ is not a neighbour of $w$ along $C$, so $g$ is a chord. The
  two arcs of $C$ between $w$ and $z$ have $a$ and $b$ edges, where
  $a + b = m$ and $a, b \ge 2$. The arc with $a$ edges, together with $g$,
  forms a cycle on $a + 1$ vertices, and $3 \le a + 1 \le m - 1$. So that
  cycle does not pass through every vertex, which contradicts
  \cref{lem:pencil-short-cycle-spanning}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Closed neighbourhoods at girth five]
  \label{lem:pencil-closed-nbhd-girth-five}
  \lean{Graph.ncard_closedNbhd_inter_le_two_of_girthGE}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:girth, def:pencil-nondegenerate}
  Let $G$ be a multigraph of girth at least five and $v \ne h$ two of its
  vertices. Then the closed neighbourhoods of $v$ and $h$
  (\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}) share at most two vertices.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Suppose they share three vertices. If $v$ and $h$ are adjacent, they
  have a common neighbour $x$, and $v, h, x$ form a cycle on three
  vertices. Otherwise they have two common neighbours $x \ne y$, and
  $v, x, h, y$ form a cycle on four vertices. In either case the edges are
  distinct, because their pairs of ends are. Both cycles contradict girth
  at least five.
\end{proof}

We find the chain through $v$ by extending a path along vertices of degree
two, first in one direction and then in the other.

\begin{lemma}[Extending a degree-two chain]
  \label{lem:pencil-chain-walk-extension}
  \lean{Graph.exists_cycleData_or_closed_or_terminated_of_twoEdgeConnected}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cut-edges-2ec, def:cycle-data}
  Let $G$ be a simple two-edge-connected multigraph and
  $P = x_0 x_1 \dots x_k$ a path with $k \ge 1$ whose vertices
  $x_1, \dots, x_k$ all have degree two. Then one of the following holds:
  $G$ is a cycle (\cref{def:cycle-data}); $P$ extends to a closed walk
  $x_0 x_1 \dots x_k \dots x_0$ returning to $x_0$, where $x_0$ has degree
  at least three and all other vertices of the walk are distinct and of
  degree two; or $P$ extends to a path $x_0 x_1 \dots x_k \dots x_\ell$
  whose interior vertices have degree two and whose last vertex $x_\ell$
  has degree at least three.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cut-edges-2ec, def:cycle-data}
  We extend $P$ one vertex at a time, by induction on $|V(G)| - k$. This
  number is positive, as the $k + 1$ vertices of $P$ are distinct. The vertex
  $x_k$ has degree two, so it has a second edge, to a vertex $y$.

  Suppose first that $y$ is not on $P$. If $y$ has degree at least three,
  the path $x_0 \dots x_k y$ is the third alternative. Otherwise $y$ has
  degree two, as every vertex of a two-edge-connected multigraph has
  degree at least two (\cref{def:cut-edges-2ec}). The path
  $x_0 \dots x_k y$ then satisfies the hypothesis, and we continue from it.

  Suppose next that $y$ is on $P$. The vertices $x_1, \dots, x_{k-1}$
  already have both of their edges on $P$, and $y = x_k$ would give a
  loop. So $y = x_0$, and $k \ge 2$, since for $k = 1$ the second edge of
  $x_1$ would be parallel to the first. If $x_0$ has degree two, every
  vertex of the closed walk has both of its edges on the walk. Then no
  edge leaves the walk's vertex set. As $G$ is connected, the walk is all
  of $G$, and $G$ is a cycle. Otherwise $x_0$ has degree at least
  three, and the closed walk is the second alternative.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The maximal degree-two chain through the split vertex]
  \label{lem:pencil-degree-two-chain}
  \lean{Graph.cycleData_or_hubLollipop_or_hubChain_of_degree_two_pair}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cut-edges-2ec, def:cycle-data, def:pencil-nondegenerate}
  Let $G$ be a simple two-edge-connected multigraph, $v$ a vertex of degree
  two whose two edges $e_a \ne e_b$ join it to $a$ and $b$, and suppose $a$
  or $b$ has degree two. Then one of the following holds:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $G$ is a cycle (\cref{def:cycle-data});
    \item there is a closed walk $w u_1 \dots u_m w$ with $m \ge 2$ through
      a pencil hub $w$, whose vertices $u_1, \dots, u_m$ are distinct, of
      degree two, and include $v$;
    \item there is a path $w u_1 \dots u_m w'$ with $m \ge 2$ between two
      distinct pencil hubs $w \ne w'$, whose interior vertices
      $u_1, \dots, u_m$ have degree two and include $v$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:pencil-chain-walk-extension}
  Say $a$ has degree two, and apply \cref{lem:pencil-chain-walk-extension}
  to the path $v a$. Its closed-walk alternative would return to $v$, which
  has degree two. So, unless $G$ is a cycle, it gives a path $v a \dots w'$
  ending at a hub $w'$. Apply the lemma again to the reversed path
  $w' \dots a v$, extending at $v$ along $e_b$. The closed-walk alternative
  returns to $w'$ and is case~(2), with $w = w'$. The path alternative ends
  at a second hub $w$, distinct from $w'$ as a path has distinct ends, and
  is case~(3). In both cases $v$ and $a$ are interior vertices, so
  $m \ge 2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The side of a degree-two chain]
  \label{lem:pencil-chain-side-connected}
  \lean{Graph.connected_deleteVerts_interior_of_twoEdgeConnected,
    Graph.degree_deleteVerts_interior_add_one}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cut-edges-2ec}
  Let $G$ be a loopless two-edge-connected multigraph and
  $P = w u_1 \dots u_m w'$ a path with $m \ge 1$ whose interior vertices
  $u_1, \dots, u_m$ have degree two. Then the multigraph
  $G - \{u_1, \dots, u_m\}$ is connected, and $w$ and $w'$ each have degree
  exactly one less in it than in $G$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:cut-edges-2ec}
  Every edge at an interior vertex is one of its two chain edges. Hence a
  connected component of $G - \{u_1, \dots, u_m\}$ containing neither $w$
  nor $w'$ is left by no edge of $G$, and a component containing exactly
  one of $w, w'$, taken together with the chain, is left by exactly one
  edge; both contradict two-edge-connectivity. So $w$ and $w'$ lie in the
  same component, which is therefore all of $G - \{u_1, \dots, u_m\}$. The
  only edge at $w$ into the chain is $w u_1$, as a second one would give
  some $u_i$ degree three. So exactly one edge at $w$ is lost, and likewise
  at $w'$.
\end{proof}
\begin{fmlnote}
  \label{fmlnote:pencil-chain-side-connected}
  The formalization states the degree count only at the end $w$. The
  count at $w'$ follows by reversing the path, which keeps its interior
  vertices.
\end{fmlnote}

\begin{lemma}[Side paths between the chain ends are long]
  \label{lem:pencil-chain-side-distance}
  \lean{Graph.le_length_add_length_of_isPath_deleteVerts_interior_of_noRigid,
    Graph.le_length_add_eDist_deleteVerts_interior_of_noRigid}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:girth, def:rigid-subgraph}
  Let $G$ be a simple multigraph with no proper rigid subgraph,
  $D = \binom{n+1}{2} \ge 3$, and $P = w u_1 \dots u_m w'$ a path with
  $m \ge 1$ whose interior vertices have degree two, where $w$ has degree at
  least three. Then every path from $w$ to $w'$ in $G - \{u_1, \dots, u_m\}$
  has at least $D - m$ edges; in particular $w$ and $w'$ are not adjacent
  when $m \le D - 2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:pencil-girth-of-hub}
  A path $Q$ from $w$ to $w'$ avoiding $u_1, \dots, u_m$ closes with $P$
  into a cycle on $m + 1 + |E(Q)|$ vertices. That cycle has at least
  $D + 1$ vertices (\cref{lem:pencil-girth-of-hub}), so
  $|E(Q)| \ge D - m$. An edge $w w'$ would be such a path with one edge,
  which needs $m \ge D - 1$.
\end{proof}
