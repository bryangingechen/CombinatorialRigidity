\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\textbf{Caution.} This blueprint is part of an ongoing
\emph{experiment} in autoformalization using LLMs. The human author
(Bryan) has not yet fully vetted the prose or the Lean code, so take
everything you read here with a grain of salt.

This blueprint accompanies the
\href{https://github.com/bryangingechen/CombinatorialRigidity}{\texttt{CombinatorialRigidity}}
Lean~4 / mathlib4 formalization. It begins with \textbf{Laman's
theorem}~\cite{pollaczekGeiringer1927,laman1970}: for $n \ge 2$, a graph
$G$ on $n$ vertices is generically rigid in the plane if and only if it
has a spanning subgraph $H$ with $2n - 3$ edges in which every set $S$
of at least two vertices spans at most $2|S| - 3$ edges. Such a graph
$H$ is a \emph{Laman graph}.
In an equivalent form, the theorem says that the Laman graphs are exactly
the graphs that are generically minimally rigid in the plane.
Pollaczek-Geiringer~\cite{pollaczekGeiringer1927} stated and proved it
in this form in 1927 (pp.~60 and~64), and Laman~\cite{laman1970} proved
it again in 1970. We keep the theorem's usual name.

To prove that every Laman graph is generically rigid in the plane, we use
the Henneberg moves. The Type~I move adds a vertex of degree two, and the
Type~II move deletes an edge $ab$ and adds a vertex joined to $a$, $b$
and a third vertex. Every Laman graph is built from $K_2$ by these moves,
and the moves preserve rigidity in the plane. Pollaczek-Geiringer's
proof is this induction: it removes a vertex of degree two or three and
puts it back by one of the moves (pp.~64--68). Laman's own proof uses the
moves implicitly, as Tay and Whiteley~\cite{tayWhiteley1985} observe
(p.~39). Their \S3 makes the argument explicit, and we follow the
presentation of Jord\'an~\cite{jordan2016} (\S2.2). For the converse, a
graph on $n$ vertices that is generically rigid in the plane has
$2n - 3$ edges whose rows in the rigidity map are linearly independent at
a suitable framework. A bound on the rank of the rigidity map by the
trivial motions shows that these edges form a Laman graph. For background
on combinatorial rigidity, see~\cite{graverServatiusServatius1993,jordan2016}.

Beyond Laman's theorem, the blueprint proves results on the rigidity
matroid in the plane, Lee and Streinu's pebble game for sparse graphs, the
union of matroids, and the theorems of Tay on body-bar frameworks and of
Tay and Whiteley on body-hinge frameworks. A body-hinge framework in
$\R^d$ joins rigid bodies in pairs by hinges, which are affine subspaces of
dimension $d - 2$. It is a \emph{panel-hinge} framework if the hinges at
each body lie in a common hyperplane, the \emph{panel} of the body. The
\emph{molecular conjecture} of Tay and Whiteley~\cite{tayWhiteley1984}
states that a graph has an infinitesimally rigid body-hinge realization if
and only if it has an infinitesimally rigid panel-hinge realization. Katoh
and Tanigawa proved it~\cite{katohTanigawa2011}. They derive it from their
Theorem~5.6: every graph has a panel-hinge realization whose rigidity
matrix has the largest rank of any body-hinge realization of the graph.
Following their proof, we prove Theorem~5.6 and the molecular conjecture
in every dimension $d \ge 2$ and over every infinite field, for simple
graphs and for multigraphs (\cref{thm:molecular-conjecture},
\cref{thm:molecular-conjecture-multigraph}). For simple graphs, the
formal statements leave out two conditions. A formal panel-hinge
framework records, for each edge, a pair of bodies whose panels meet in
its hinge, and neither statement requires the pair to be the ends of the
edge (\cref{fmlnote:panel-hinge-nondegenerate}). The statement of
Theorem~5.6 also allows degenerate hinges, so for a graph of deficiency
zero (\cref{def:D-deficiency}) a framework whose normals are all zero
meets its conclusion: each hinge welds the two bodies of its edge, and
such a graph is connected. The form with nondegenerate hinges, each the
meet of the panels of the ends of its edge, is proved for simple graphs
with at least two vertices (\cref{thm:theorem-55-6-genuine}), and the
proof of \cref{thm:molecular-conjecture} gives the panel side of the
conjecture in this form. The statements for multigraphs place each
hinge in the panels of the ends of its edge.

From Theorem~5.6, Katoh and Tanigawa derive the \emph{molecule rank
formula} (\cref{cor:molecule-rank-formula}), which Jackson and Jord\'an
conjectured. It gives the rank of the square $G^2$ of a graph $G$ of
minimum degree at least two in the generic rigidity matroid in $\R^3$.
When $G$ is the graph of the atoms and bonds of a molecule, $G^2$ is the
graph of its bar-joint model. Jackson and Jord\'an derive from the formula
Jacobs' conjecture on the independence of squares of graphs. A later
chapter proves this conjecture and their degree-one rank formula
(\cref{sec:jacobs}). Two
later chapters (\cref{sec:pencil}, \cref{sec:main-component}) prove a
strengthening of Katoh--Tanigawa's Theorem~5.6 in dimension three that
appears to be new, the pencil realization theorem, which this project
posed and proved. Its realizations ask in addition that the hinges at each
body pass through a common point of the body's panel.

\subsection*{Organization of the blueprint}

The Lean development is organized into phases, tracked in
\href{https://github.com/bryangingechen/CombinatorialRigidity/blob/master/ROADMAP.md}{\texttt{ROADMAP.md}},
which also lists the Lean files of each phase. Phases~1--26 form four
arcs:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Laman's theorem} (phases~1--6): sparse graphs, Laman
        graphs, Henneberg moves, frameworks, and the two directions of
        the theorem.
  \item \textbf{The planar rigidity matroid and the pebble game}
        (phases~7--11): the rigidity matroid in the plane, as a count
        matroid and as a linear matroid, and the $(k,\ell)$-pebble game,
        a computable test for sparsity that returns a certificate with
        its answer.
  \item \textbf{Body-bar and body-hinge rigidity} (phases~12--16):
        matroid union, Tay's theorem on body-bar frameworks, and the
        theorem of Tay and Whiteley on body-hinge frameworks.
  \item \textbf{The molecular conjecture} (phases~17--26): Katoh and
        Tanigawa's proof of the molecular
        conjecture~\cite{katohTanigawa2011}, cited as KT below, and the
        rank formula for squares of graphs that they derive from it.
\end{itemize}

The phases of the four arcs, with the chapters they contribute to:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sparsity.} $(k,\ell)$-sparse and $(k,\ell)$-tight graphs,
        for all $k$ and $\ell$ (\cref{sec:edgesIn}, \cref{sec:sparsity}).
  \item \textbf{Laman graphs.} The case $(k,\ell) = (2,3)$, $K_2$ as the
        only Laman graph on two vertices, and the degrees of the vertices
        of a Laman graph (\cref{sec:laman}).
  \item \textbf{Henneberg moves.} The two moves, which preserve the Laman
        property, and the decomposition of every Laman graph on at least
        three vertices by a reverse move (\cref{sec:henneberg}).
  \item \textbf{Frameworks.} The rigidity map, and infinitesimal and
        generic rigidity (\cref{sec:frameworks}).
  \item \textbf{Laman's theorem ($\Leftarrow$).} The Henneberg moves
        preserve rigidity in the plane, so every Laman graph is
        generically rigid there (\cref{sec:henneberg-rigidity},
        \cref{sec:laman-theorem}).
  \item \textbf{Laman's theorem ($\Rightarrow$).} The trivial motions
        bound the rank of the rigidity map, and the bound shows that a
        generically rigid graph in the plane has a spanning Laman subgraph
        (\cref{sec:trivial-motions}, \cref{sec:laman-theorem}).
  \item \textbf{Row independence and the count matroid.} Every
        $(2,3)$-sparse edge set is row-independent at some framework in
        the plane, which completes Pollaczek-Geiringer's and Laman's
        characterization of the planar rigidity matroid; and the
        $(k,\ell)$-count matroid of Lorea and of White and Whiteley, for
        $\ell < 2k$ (\cref{sec:rigidity-matroid}, \cref{sec:count-matroid}).
  \item \textbf{Linear-matroid framing.} The planar rigidity matroid as
        the rigidity matroid of one framework of the complete graph
        (\cref{sec:rigidity-matroid-linear-framing}).
  \item \textbf{Pebble game.} Lee and Streinu's $(k,\ell)$-pebble game
        for $\ell < 2k$, which generalizes Jacobs and Hendrickson's: on a
        simple graph it accepts every edge if and only if the graph is
        $(k,\ell)$-sparse (\cref{sec:dfs}, \cref{sec:pebble-game}).
  \item \textbf{Executable pebble game.} A computable form of the game,
        procedures that decide $(k,\ell)$-sparsity, $(k,\ell)$-tightness
        and the Laman property, and a command-line program that decides
        whether a graph read from a file is Laman
        (\cref{sec:executable}).
  \item \textbf{Witness extraction.} The game returns a certificate with
        its answer: the orientation it builds when it accepts, and
        otherwise a set $V'$ of vertices spanning more than
        $k|V'| - \ell$ edges. The command-line program prints it
        (\cref{sec:pebble-game-verdict}, \cref{sec:executable-cli}).
  \item \textbf{Matroid union.} The matroid of a submodular function
        (Edmonds and Rota), Rado's theorem, and the union of matroids with
        its rank (Nash-Williams and Edmonds) and Edmonds' partition
        theorem, after Oxley; the formal proofs are ported from Peter
        Nelson's matroid package for Lean (\cref{sec:matroid-union}).
  \item \textbf{Tree packing.} The theorem of Tutte and Nash-Williams: a
        multigraph is the union of $k$ disjoint forests if and only if it
        is $(k,k)$-sparse (\cref{sec:body-bar-matroid-union},
        \cref{sec:body-bar-tree-packing}).
  \item \textbf{The $k$-frame.} Whiteley's Theorem~1: the general
        $k$-frame of a multigraph, a matrix of indeterminates, represents
        the union of $k$ copies of its cycle matroid
        (\cref{sec:body-bar-k-frame}).
  \item \textbf{Tay's theorem.} Let $d \ge 2$ and $D = \binom{d+1}{2}$. A
        multigraph has an independent body-bar framework in $\R^d$ if and
        only if it is $(D,D)$-sparse, and an independent and
        infinitesimally rigid one if and only if it is $(D,D)$-tight
        (\cref{sec:body-bar-framework}, \cref{sec:body-bar-tay}).
  \item \textbf{Body-hinge frameworks.} The theorem of Tay and Whiteley,
        proved with each hinge replaced by $D - 1$ bars: a multigraph $G$
        has an independent and infinitesimally rigid such framework in
        $\R^d$ if and only if $(D-1)\cdot G$, with each edge repeated
        $D - 1$ times, is $(D,D)$-tight (\cref{sec:body-hinge}).
  \item \textbf{Extensors.} Homogeneous coordinates, extensors and their
        join, and KT Lemma~2.1: the extensors of the $D$ sets of $d - 1$
        of $d + 1$ affinely independent points are linearly independent
        (\cref{sec:molecular}).
  \item \textbf{The panel-hinge rigidity matrix.} The rigidity matrix
        $R(G,p)$ of a body-hinge framework, built from the extensors of
        its hinges (KT \S\S2.2--2.4), and KT Lemmas~5.1--5.3 on its rank
        (\cref{sec:molecular-rigidity-matrix}).
  \item \textbf{Deficiency and $k$-dof-graphs.} The matroid
        $M(\tilde G)$ of the multigraph $\tilde G = (D-1)\cdot G$, the
        deficiency $\operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$, $k$-dof-graphs and
        rigid subgraphs; KT eq.~(2.4), which relates the deficiency to the
        rank of $M(\tilde G)$; and the lemmas of KT~\S3
        (\cref{sec:molecular-deficiency}).
  \item \textbf{Combinatorial induction.} Splitting off, edge-splitting
        and the contraction of rigid subgraphs, the lemmas of KT~\S4, and
        KT Theorem~4.9: every minimal $0$-dof-graph with at least two
        vertices reduces to the two-vertex double edge
        (\cref{sec:molecular-induction}).
  \item \textbf{Algebraic induction: the first cases.} The
        Grassmann--Cayley meet (\cref{sec:molecular-meet}), panel-hinge
        frameworks, and the base and Cases~I and~II of KT Theorem~5.5,
        whose proof is an induction over minimal $k$-dof-graphs of every
        deficiency $k$ (\cref{sec:molecular-algebraic-induction}).
  \item \textbf{Cases~I and~III.} KT Claim~6.4 of Case~I, KT Claim~6.11
        of Case~III, and KT Lemma~6.10 with KT Claim~6.12, the case
        $d = 3$ of Case~III (\cref{sec:molecular-algebraic-induction-caseI},
        \cref{sec:molecular-algebraic-induction-caseIII}).
  \item \textbf{Case~III in general dimension.} KT Lemma~6.13, which
        completes KT Theorem~5.5 for $d \ge 3$. With it come the rank
        formula of Jackson and Jord\'an (KT Proposition~2.3), and KT
        Theorem~5.6 and the molecular conjecture for simple graphs
        (\cref{thm:molecular-conjecture}).
  \item \textbf{The generic bar-joint rigidity matroid.} The generic
        rigidity matroid $\mathcal{R}_d(V)$ in every dimension $d$, as the
        rigidity matroid of the complete graph at a placement that is
        generic for row independence, and its rank function
        (\cref{sec:bar-joint-3d}).
  \item \textbf{Projective duality and the molecule modelling
        equivalence.} Projective invariance of infinitesimal rigidity
        (Crapo and Whiteley), under a polarity that turns molecular
        frameworks into panel-hinge frameworks, and Whiteley's equivalence
        between molecular frameworks on a graph $G$ and bar-joint frameworks
        on its square $G^2$, both as equalities of dimensions of motion
        spaces (\cref{sec:molecule-modelling}).
  \item \textbf{The molecule rank formula.} KT Corollary~5.7, which
        Jackson and Jord\'an conjectured: for a graph $G$ of minimum degree
        at least two, $r(G^2) = 3|V| - 6 - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$,
        where $r$ is the rank function of the generic rigidity matroid in
        $\R^3$ (\cref{sec:molecule-application}).
\end{enumerate}

Beyond the four arcs, a continuation (phase~32) proves two results of
Jackson and Jord\'an~\cite{jacksonJordan2008} on squares of graphs
(\cref{sec:jacobs}). The first is Jacobs' conjecture: the square of a
graph is independent in the generic rigidity matroid in $\R^3$ if and
only if it satisfies the three-dimensional Laman condition of Jackson and
Jord\'an. They derived it from the molecule rank formula, so the
conjecture holds. The second is their degree-one rank formula, for
connected graphs with vertices of degree one, to which the molecule rank
formula does not apply. It gives the rank of the square of a tree
explicitly, a case that Jackson and Jord\'an credit to Franzblau. For
any other such graph it gives the rank of the square through that of the
square of the core, the maximal subgraph of minimum degree at least two.

A second continuation (phase~33) proves KT Theorems~5.5 and~5.6 and the
molecular conjecture over every infinite field $K$, of any
characteristic. Katoh and Tanigawa's statements over $\R$ are the case
$K = \R$. For these theorems the generality appears to be new. For
body-bar frameworks there is a precedent: Whiteley states the genericity
step of his proof of Tay's theorem over every infinite
field~\cite{whiteley1988}. The opening of
\cref{sec:molecular-algebraic-induction} discusses this generality. The
molecule rank formula, a statement about the generic rigidity matroid in
$\R^3$, stays over $\R$.

A third continuation (phase~34) proves the realization theorems above in
generic form, by the coordinate argument of Jackson and
Jord\'an~\cite{jacksonJordan2009} (\cref{sec:generic-lift}). Each
theorem holds at every generic realization, and the realizations that
are not generic lie in the zero set of a nonzero polynomial. The
theorems are the molecule rank formula, at generic placements; Tay's
theorem, at generic endpoints of the bars; and, for a simple graph, the
rank formula of KT Proposition~2.3, at generic normals of the panels and
at generic points spanning the hinges. The last gives the theorem of Tay
and Whiteley for frameworks with hinges, which phase~16 proves for
bundles of bars. As formalized, both forms of the rank formula assume
that every edge label is an edge of the graph, and also the supply of
unused labels of \cref{rem:fresh-edge-supply}. The two assumptions
contradict each other when the graph is itself a minimal
$k$-dof-graph, such as a single edge, a tree or a minimal
$0$-dof-graph, so these theorems say nothing about such a graph. They
apply to every graph with more than $D(|V| - 1)$ edges, where
$D = \binom{d+1}{2}$, for instance to $K_7$ at $d = 2$. Which graphs
in between they cover is not settled.

A fourth continuation (phase~35) proves KT Theorem~5.6 and the molecular
conjecture for multigraphs, as Katoh and Tanigawa state them
(\cref{sec:molecular-coplanar-multigraph}). It works in their own model,
the hinge-coplanar model, in which each hinge need only lie in the
panels of the two bodies it joins. In the panel-hinge frameworks of the
main development, a hinge is the intersection of the two panels. For
these frameworks the multigraph form of the molecular conjecture fails.

A fifth continuation (phases~39--40) strengthens the multigraph form of
Theorem~5.6 in the hinge-coplanar model, in dimension three. A
\emph{pencil realization} gives each body of a hinge-coplanar realization
a point of its panel, through which every hinge at the body passes
(\cref{def:pencil-panel-realization}). The hinges at a body then form a
pencil of lines through a point in a plane. In the molecular reading, the
panel is the plane of an atom's bonds and the point is the atom's centre.
Over every infinite field, every spanning multigraph $G$ has a pencil
realization of rank $6(|V(G)| - 1) - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$, the
deficiency rank of \cref{thm:theorem-55-6-multigraph}. If $G$ is simple,
the points of adjacent bodies can be chosen projectively distinct. If $G$
also has a nondegenerate pencil realization
(\cref{def:pencil-nondegenerate}), it has one of that rank. These claims
make up the pencil realization theorem (\cref{thm:pencil-realization-theorem}).

Katoh and Tanigawa's induction runs over minimal graphs (KT
Theorem~4.9). They then add the remaining edges to a realization of a
minimal spanning subgraph. In a pencil realization of the subgraph at its
deficiency rank, the added edges may have no hinges in both panels
through the points of their ends. So the proof runs the induction over
every multigraph (\cref{thm:pencil-reduction}). Phase~39 reduced the
pencil realization theorem to the two \emph{main-component statements}
(\cref{def:pencil-main-component-statements}). These ask the pencil
realization theorem's last two claims of every simple two-edge-connected
multigraph on at least three bodies.

Phase~40 proved the two statements (\cref{sec:main-component}). The idea
is to build pencil realizations by lifting pictures of the graph in the
plane. A picture places each body $v$ at a point $q_v = (x_v, y_v)$ of
the plane. Lifting that point to a height $z_v$ gives the point
$p_v = (x_v, y_v, z_v, 1)$ of $K^4$. The hinge of an edge $uv$ is the line
through $p_u$ and $p_v$. The configurations at which the points of every
closed neighbourhood lie in a plane form the \emph{pencil configuration
space}. Over an admissible picture $q$
(\cref{def:pencil-admissible-picture}), each of them is a pencil
realization. Their heights form a linear space $L(q)$, the lifting space
(\cref{def:pencil-lifting-space}). The \emph{main component} is the
closure of the part of the pencil configuration space over the main
pictures, the admissible pictures at which $\dim L(q)$ is least
(\cref{rem:pencil-x0-main-component}).

For every simple connected graph of minimum degree at least two, a
general configuration in the main component has the deficiency rank
(\cref{thm:pencil-x0-generic-attains}). The proof is a strong induction
on the number of bodies. There are steps at cut vertices, bridges, cycles
and ears, and steps by splitting off and by contraction. A case analysis,
the structural coverage, shows that some step applies to every such graph
(\cref{thm:pencil-x0-coverage}). Over an admissible picture adjacent
bodies have distinct points, so the first statement follows
(\cref{lem:pencil-x0-distinct-statement}). The second does not follow in
this way. At $K_{2,3}$, no configuration over a general picture is
nondegenerate (\cref{lem:pencil-x0-two-hubs-obstruction}). The second
statement is proved instead inside the induction over every multigraph
(\cref{thm:pencil-generic-step}).

The first step of phase~40 extended Theorems~5.5 and~5.6 and the
molecular conjecture to dimension two. It also proved a form of
Theorem~5.6 for simple graphs that need not span the body set
(\cref{thm:theorem-55-6-rows}). In the plane a hinge is a pin joining two
bodies, and a panel is a line. There the molecular conjecture is a
theorem of Jackson and Jord\'an on pin-collinear body-and-pin
frameworks~\cite{jacksonJordan2008pin}, proved for real frameworks. Here
it holds over every infinite field. The main-component argument uses
Jackson and Jord\'an's rank formula for these frameworks, as an equality
for the dimension of the lifting space at a general picture. We derive
that equality from the planar case of the non-spanning form of
Theorem~5.6, so that it too holds over every infinite field
(\cref{sec:main-component-jj}).

\subsection*{Reading this blueprint}

In the web version, a statement that is formalized in Lean is marked with
a tick, and a button in its heading links to the documentation of its
Lean declarations. The \emph{dependency graph}, linked from the
navigation bar, shows how the statements depend on each other. A node has
a green border when its statement is formalized, and a green interior
when its proof is; the graph's legend explains the other markings. The
main results of every chapter are formalized, from Laman's theorem to
the pencil realization theorem (\cref{sec:pencil}, \cref{sec:main-component}).
From phase~6 on, the graph is written forward: a chapter's nodes and
their edges come first, and the statement and proof sketch of a node not
yet formalized are the plan for its Lean declaration. So the graph is
also the project's list of work to do, and its legend marks the nodes
whose prerequisites are all formalized. Outside this introduction and the
appendix (\cref{sec:formalization-notes}), the chapters state the
mathematics, not the progress of its formalization.

The proofs give the argument as a paper would, and the Lean proofs
behind the links give every detail. The chapters on the molecular
conjecture give in full the steps that Katoh and Tanigawa's paper states
compactly, so that each of them can be followed without the paper. Among
these steps are the cases of KT Theorem~5.5, of which Katoh and Tanigawa
call Case~III the most difficult (KT p.~650). Where the Lean statement of
a node differs from the statement in the text, for instance in how it
encodes a construction, a formalization note after the node explains the
difference.

An appendix (\cref{sec:formalization-notes}) records some of the wrong
turns that the formalization of KT Theorem~5.5 took: a statement whose
formal strength differed from the source's, a decomposition of a proof
that did not match the structure the final argument needed, and a
decision left with no record of the obligation it created. It stands
outside the mathematical development and can be read on its own.
