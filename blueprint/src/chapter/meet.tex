\section{Grassmann--Cayley meet and projective duality}
\label{sec:molecular-meet}

This chapter defines the meet, the operation of the Grassmann--Cayley
algebra dual to the join, and proves that for $d = 3$ the meet of two
planes is a scalar multiple of the join of two points of their line of
intersection. Katoh and Tanigawa use the join and not the meet. For the
Grassmann--Cayley algebra they refer to Barnabei, Brini and
Rota~\cite{barnabeiBriniRota1985} and to White~\cite{white1994}, among
others~\cite[\S2.1]{katohTanigawa2011}. They define the extensor of an
affine subspace through points that span it, and in a nonparallel
panel-hinge framework the hinge of an edge $uv$ is the intersection
$\Pi(u) \cap \Pi(v)$ of the panels of its ends~\cite[\S5.1]{katohTanigawa2011}.
The formalization records such a hinge by the meet of the two panels
(\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}). For $d = 3$,
\cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union} shows that this meet
agrees with the join of two points of the hinge up to a scalar. This is
what lets the proof of KT Lemma~6.10, which spans $\bigwedge^2 \R^4$ by
joins of points in KT eq.~(6.45)~\cite[p.~691]{katohTanigawa2011}, apply
to hinges given by panels.

The chapter builds only on \cref{sec:molecular-extensor}: the exterior
algebra of $V = K^{d+1}$, its extensors, and the join. As there, $K$ is
an arbitrary field. The constructions use the standard basis of $V$,
through a volume form and a coordinate pairing, and no order on $K$ or
hypothesis on its characteristic. We write $\vee$ for the product of an
exterior algebra, as in \cref{def:join}, and $\wedge$ for the meet, as
Barnabei, Brini and Rota and White do.

The first subsection defines the volume form (\cref{def:meet-top-equiv}).
It then defines the duality between the exterior powers of $V$ and of its
dual space (\cref{def:meet-pairing-dual}). The pairing
$(X, Y) \mapsto \operatorname{vol}(X \vee Y)$ is nondegenerate, and it
gives the complement isomorphism $*$ (\cref{def:meet-complement-iso}),
whose defining property is \cref{lem:complement-iso-toDual}. The meet is
then $A \wedge B = *({*A} \vee {*B})$ (\cref{def:meet}), and the meet of
two hyperplanes is an extensor of their intersection
(\cref{lem:meet-two-hyperplanes}). The second
subsection takes $d = 3$ and proves the agreement of meet and join
(\cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union}). It ends with the
converse fact that a nonzero $2$-extensor determines its plane
(\cref{lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}).

\subsection{The complement isomorphism and the meet}

Let $V = K^{d+1}$, with standard basis $e_1, \dots, e_{d+1}$. For a set
$S = \{s_1 < \cdots < s_i\} \subseteq \{1, \dots, d+1\}$ we write
$e_S = e_{s_1} \vee \cdots \vee e_{s_i}$. The products $e_S$ over the
$i$-element sets $S$ form a basis of $\bigwedge^i V$ (the remark after
\cref{def:plucker-coords}), and $X_S$ denotes the coordinate of
$X \in \bigwedge^i V$ at $e_S$. For $i = d+1$ there is one such product.

\begin{definition}[The volume form $\bigwedge^{d+1} V \cong K$]
  \label{def:meet-top-equiv}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.screwAlgebraTopEquiv,
    exteriorPower.topEquiv}
  \leanok
  The \emph{volume form}
  $\operatorname{vol} \colon \bigwedge^{d+1} V \to K$ is the linear
  isomorphism with $\operatorname{vol}(e_1 \vee \cdots \vee e_{d+1}) = 1$.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The space $\bigwedge^{d+1} V$ has dimension $\binom{d+1}{d+1} = 1$,
  with basis $e_1 \vee \cdots \vee e_{d+1}$. The volume form takes an
  element to its coordinate in this basis.
\end{proof}
\begin{fmlnote}
  The second pin is the same isomorphism for $R^n$ over a commutative
  ring $R$, and the first is its case $R = K$, $n = d+1$. As in
  \cref{sec:molecular-rigidity-matrix}, the formalization writes
  $d = k + 1$, so that $V = K^{k+2}$.
\end{fmlnote}

By the expansion after \cref{def:plucker-coords},
$\operatorname{vol}(v_1 \vee \cdots \vee v_{d+1})$ is the determinant of
the matrix with rows $v_1, \dots, v_{d+1}$.

The exterior powers of $V$ and of its dual space $V^*$ are dual to each
other through determinants.

\begin{definition}[Duality of exterior powers $\bigwedge^{j} V^{*} \cong (\bigwedge^{j} V)^{*}$]
  \label{def:meet-pairing-dual}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.screwAlgebraPairingDualEquiv,
    exteriorPower.pairingDualEquiv}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:meet-top-equiv}
  Let $j \ge 0$. The linear map
  $\bigwedge^{j} V^{*} \to (\bigwedge^{j} V)^{*}$ that sends
  $\varphi_1 \vee \cdots \vee \varphi_j$ to the linear form
  \[
    v_1 \vee \cdots \vee v_j \;\longmapsto\;
    \det\bigl(\varphi_a(v_b)\bigr)_{a,b = 1}^{j}
  \]
  is an isomorphism.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Let $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_{d+1}$ be the basis of $V^*$ dual to
  $e_1, \dots, e_{d+1}$, and let $\varphi_S$ be the products of its
  elements, as for $e_S$. For $j$-element sets $S$ and $T$, the matrix
  $(\varphi_{s_a}(e_{t_b}))$ is the identity if $S = T$, and it has a
  zero row otherwise. So the map sends $\varphi_S$ to the linear form
  $X \mapsto X_S$. It takes the basis $(\varphi_S)$ of
  $\bigwedge^j V^*$ to the basis of $(\bigwedge^j V)^*$ dual to
  $(e_S)$, and is therefore an isomorphism.
\end{proof}
\begin{fmlnote}
  The second pin is the isomorphism for a finite free module with an
  ordered basis over a commutative ring, built from that basis as in the
  proof, and the first is its case $V = K^{d+1}$ with the standard
  basis.
\end{fmlnote}

The complement isomorphism compares two pairings. For each $i$, the
\emph{coordinate pairing} on $\bigwedge^i V$ is the bilinear form
$\langle X, Y \rangle = \sum_S X_S Y_S$, for which the basis $(e_S)$ is
orthonormal. On $V = \bigwedge^1 V$ it is the dot product. It identifies
$\bigwedge^i V$ with its dual, as $Z \mapsto \langle Z, \cdot \rangle$
is an isomorphism $\bigwedge^i V \to (\bigwedge^i V)^*$. The second
pairing uses the volume form. For $0 \le j \le d+1$, the join of
$X \in \bigwedge^j V$ and $Y \in \bigwedge^{d+1-j} V$ lies in
$\bigwedge^{d+1} V$, and the \emph{wedge pairing} of $X$ and $Y$ is
$\operatorname{vol}(X \vee Y)$.

\begin{definition}[The complement isomorphism $\bigwedge^{j} V \cong \bigwedge^{d+1-j} V$]
  \label{def:meet-complement-iso}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.complementIso}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:meet-top-equiv}
  Let $0 \le j \le d+1$. The wedge pairing
  $\bigwedge^{j} V \times \bigwedge^{d+1-j} V \to K$ is nondegenerate,
  so $X \mapsto \operatorname{vol}(X \vee \cdot)$ is an isomorphism
  $\bigwedge^{j} V \to (\bigwedge^{d+1-j} V)^*$. The \emph{complement
  isomorphism} $* \colon \bigwedge^{j} V \to \bigwedge^{d+1-j} V$ is this
  isomorphism followed by the inverse of the coordinate identification
  of $\bigwedge^{d+1-j} V$ with its dual.
\end{definition}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Let $S$ be a $j$-element set and $T$ a $(d+1-j)$-element set in
  $\{1, \dots, d+1\}$. The join $e_S \vee e_T$ is the extensor of the
  family that lists $e_s$ for $s \in S$ and then $e_t$ for $t \in T$
  (\cref{def:join}). If $S$ and $T$ meet, this family repeats a vector,
  and $e_S \vee e_T = 0$ (the remark after \cref{def:extensor}). Since
  $|S| + |T| = d+1$, the sets $S$ and $T$ are disjoint exactly when $T$
  is the complement $S^c$ of $S$. In that case the family lists each
  standard basis vector once. It is linearly independent, so
  $e_S \vee e_{S^c} \neq 0$, and
  $\varepsilon_S = \operatorname{vol}(e_S \vee e_{S^c}) \neq 0$, as the
  volume form is injective. In the bases $(e_S)$ and $(e_T)$, the matrix
  of the wedge pairing therefore has exactly one nonzero entry in each row
  and each column: $\varepsilon_S$, in row $S$ and column $S^c$.

  Hence $X \mapsto \operatorname{vol}(X \vee \cdot)$ is injective. If
  $\operatorname{vol}(X \vee Y) = 0$ for every $Y$, then $Y = e_{S^c}$
  gives $\varepsilon_S X_S = 0$, so $X_S = 0$ for every $S$, and $X = 0$.
  The spaces $\bigwedge^{j} V$ and $(\bigwedge^{d+1-j} V)^*$ both have
  dimension $\binom{d+1}{j} = \binom{d+1}{d+1-j}$, so this injective
  linear map is an isomorphism. The coordinate identification of
  $\bigwedge^{d+1-j} V$ with its dual is an isomorphism because the basis
  $(e_T)$ is orthonormal, and $*$ is the first isomorphism followed by
  the inverse of the second.

  In fact $\varepsilon_S = \pm 1$ is the sign of the permutation that
  lists the elements of $S$ and then those of $S^c$, each in increasing
  order. The argument needs only $\varepsilon_S \neq 0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The defining property of the complement isomorphism]
  \label{lem:complement-iso-toDual}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.complementIso_toDual}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:meet-complement-iso, def:meet-top-equiv}
  Let $0 \le j \le d+1$, $X \in \bigwedge^{j} V$ and
  $Y \in \bigwedge^{d+1-j} V$. Then
  \[
    \langle *X, Y \rangle = \operatorname{vol}(X \vee Y).
  \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The coordinate identification sends $*X$ to the linear form
  $\langle *X, \cdot \rangle$, which is
  $\operatorname{vol}(X \vee \cdot)$ by \cref{def:meet-complement-iso}.
\end{proof}

Taking $X = e_S$ and $Y = e_T$ shows that
$\langle *e_S, e_T \rangle$ is $\varepsilon_S$ if $T = S^c$ and $0$
otherwise. So $*e_S = \varepsilon_S\, e_{S^c}$
(\texttt{complementIso\_\allowbreak{}exteriorPower\_\allowbreak{}basis\_\allowbreak{}eq\_\allowbreak{}smul\_\allowbreak{}compl}),
and $*$ is the Hodge star operator of the standard basis, which Barnabei,
Brini and Rota use to state the duality between join and
meet~\cite[p.~146]{barnabeiBriniRota1985}.

The meet is the join transported by the complement isomorphism.

\begin{definition}[The meet (regressive product)]
  \label{def:meet}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.meet}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:meet-complement-iso}
  Let $a, b \ge 0$ with $a + b \le d+1$, and let
  $A \in \bigwedge^{d+1-a} V$ and $B \in \bigwedge^{d+1-b} V$. The
  \emph{meet} of $A$ and $B$ is
  \[
    A \wedge B \;=\; *\bigl({*A} \vee {*B}\bigr)
    \;\in\; \textstyle\bigwedge^{d+1-a-b} V,
  \]
  where ${*A} \in \bigwedge^{a} V$ and ${*B} \in \bigwedge^{b} V$, so
  that their join lies in $\bigwedge^{a+b} V$.
\end{definition}
\begin{fmlnote}
  The formalization proves no property of the meet itself. It proves the
  formula for $*e_S$ and the transformation law below, and the case of
  two hyperplanes in the form $*(n_A \vee n_B)$
  (\cref{lem:meet-two-hyperplanes}), which is how the later chapters use
  the meet (\cref{def:panel-support-extensor}). The
  statements below for a general subspace $U$ and for general subspaces
  $X$ and $Y$ are not formalized.
\end{fmlnote}

The meet of extensors of two subspaces is an extensor of their
intersection. Let $U$ be a subspace of $V$, and let $U^\perp$ be its
orthogonal complement for the dot product. The complement isomorphism
sends a nonzero extensor of $U$ to a nonzero extensor of $U^\perp$. For
$U = U_S$, the span of the $e_i$ with $i \in S$, this is
$*e_S = \varepsilon_S\, e_{S^c}$. In general, write $U = g(U_S)$ for a
linear automorphism $g$ of $V$, and let $h$ be its inverse transpose, so
that $h(x) \cdot g(y) = x \cdot y$. The extensors of $U$ are the
multiples of $g(e_{s_1}) \vee \cdots \vee g(e_{s_i})$, and
\[
  *\bigl(g(e_{s_1}) \vee \cdots \vee g(e_{s_i})\bigr)
  \;=\; \det(g)\, \varepsilon_S\;
  h(e_{t_1}) \vee \cdots \vee h(e_{t_{d+1-i}}),
\]
where $S^c = \{t_1 < \cdots < t_{d+1-i}\}$
(\texttt{complementIso\_\allowbreak{}map\_\allowbreak{}contragredient\_\allowbreak{}eq}).
The right side is a nonzero extensor of $h(U_{S^c})$. This is $U^\perp$,
since $h(e_t) \cdot g(e_s) = e_t \cdot e_s = 0$ for $t \in S^c$ and
$s \in S$, and the two spaces have the same dimension.

Now let $A$ and $B$ be nonzero extensors of subspaces $X$ and $Y$ of $V$
with $X + Y = V$. Then ${*A}$ and ${*B}$ are nonzero extensors of
$X^\perp$ and $Y^\perp$, and $X^\perp \cap Y^\perp = (X + Y)^\perp = 0$.
So ${*A} \vee {*B}$ is a nonzero extensor of
$X^\perp + Y^\perp = (X \cap Y)^\perp$ (the remark after \cref{def:join}),
and $A \wedge B$ is a nonzero extensor of $X \cap Y$. Barnabei, Brini and
Rota define the meet by a sum over the splits of $A$ and prove this
property for it~\cite[\S4, Proposition~4.3]{barnabeiBriniRota1985}.
White gives the same definition as a sum over
shuffles~\cite[\S4]{white1994}.

The later chapters use the case of two distinct hyperplanes $H_A$ and
$H_B$, where $a = b = 1$. Then $n_A = {*A}$ spans $H_A^\perp$, so it is a
normal of $H_A$, and likewise $n_B = {*B}$ for $H_B$. The meet
$A \wedge B = *(n_A \vee n_B)$ is a nonzero extensor of $H_A \cap H_B$.
The next lemma states this for every $d$, in the form the formalization
proves, and \cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union} below
proves it for $d = 3$ by a count of dimensions.

\begin{lemma}[The meet of two hyperplanes is an extensor of their intersection]
  \label{lem:meet-two-hyperplanes}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.complementIso_extensor_mem_range_map_subtype,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensor_join_proportional_complementIso_meet}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:meet-complement-iso, def:join}
  Let $d \ge 1$, let $n_1, n_2 \in V$, and let $W$ be a
  $(d-1)$-dimensional subspace of $V$ whose vectors are orthogonal to
  $n_1$ and $n_2$. Then $*(n_1 \vee n_2)$ lies in the image of the map
  $\bigwedge^{d-1} W \to \bigwedge^{d-1} V$ induced by the inclusion of
  $W$ in $V$. If moreover $n_1$ and $n_2$ are linearly independent, and
  $p_1, \dots, p_{d-1} \in V$ are linearly independent and orthogonal to
  $n_1$ and $n_2$, then
  $p_1 \vee \cdots \vee p_{d-1} = c\, {*(n_1 \vee n_2)}$ for some
  $c \in K$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:complement-iso-toDual}
  If $n_1$ and $n_2$ are linearly dependent, then $n_1 \vee n_2 = 0$
  (the remark after \cref{def:extensor}), and the first statement holds
  as $*0 = 0$. Otherwise there is a linear automorphism $g$ of $V$ with
  $g(e_1) = n_1$ and $g(e_2) = n_2$. Let $h$ be its inverse transpose.
  By the formula displayed after \cref{def:meet}, with $S = \{1, 2\}$,
  which follows from \cref{lem:complement-iso-toDual},
  \[
    *(n_1 \vee n_2) \;=\; \det(g)\, \varepsilon_S\;
    h(e_3) \vee \cdots \vee h(e_{d+1}).
  \]
  For $t \ge 3$ and $i = 1, 2$ we have
  $h(e_t) \cdot n_i = h(e_t) \cdot g(e_i) = e_t \cdot e_i = 0$. So the
  vectors $h(e_t)$ lie in the orthogonal complement of $n_1$ and $n_2$.
  This complement has dimension $d - 1$ and contains $W$, so it is $W$,
  and $*(n_1 \vee n_2)$ is a multiple of a join of $d - 1$ vectors of
  $W$. Hence it lies in the image of $\bigwedge^{d-1} W$.

  For the second statement, let $W$ be the orthogonal complement of
  $n_1$ and $n_2$, of dimension $d - 1$. The space $\bigwedge^{d-1} W$
  has dimension $\binom{d-1}{d-1} = 1$, so its image in
  $\bigwedge^{d-1} V$ has dimension at most $1$. The vectors $p_i$ lie in
  $W$, so their join lies in this image, and it is nonzero, as they are
  linearly independent. The meet $*(n_1 \vee n_2)$ lies in the image by
  the first statement. So it is a multiple $\mu\, (p_1 \vee \cdots \vee
  p_{d-1})$, and $\mu \neq 0$, as $n_1 \vee n_2 \neq 0$ and $*$ is an
  isomorphism. Then $c = \mu^{-1}$ will do.
\end{proof}
\begin{fmlnote}
  The first pin is the first statement, and the second pin the second.
\end{fmlnote}

\subsection{Lines in projective three-space}

In this subsection $d = 3$ and $V = K^4$, except in the first statement
of \cref{lem:complement-iso-decomposable-wedge-perp} and in
\cref{lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}, which hold for every $d$.
A point $p \in K^3$ has the homogeneous coordinate vector
$\bar p = (p, 1) \in K^4$ (\cref{def:homogeneous-coords}). The plane
$\{x \in K^3 : a \cdot x + a_0 = 0\}$ has the normal
$n = (a, a_0) \in K^4$, and a point $p$ lies on it exactly when
$\bar p \cdot n = 0$. Let $L$ be the line in which two nonparallel planes
meet, and let $n_1$ and $n_2$ be their normals, which are linearly
independent. Let
\[
  W = \{x \in K^4 : x \cdot n_1 = x \cdot n_2 = 0\},
\]
a $2$-dimensional subspace that contains $\bar p$ for every $p \in L$.
The extensor of $L$ is $\bar p \vee \bar q$ for distinct points $p$ and
$q$ of $L$ (\cref{def:affine-subspace-extensor}). The meet of the two
planes (two panels, in a panel-hinge framework) is a scalar multiple of
$*(n_1 \vee n_2)$, a nonzero extensor of $W$ by the remarks after
\cref{def:meet} and by \cref{lem:meet-two-hyperplanes}.
\Cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union} proves
that the two extensors agree up to a scalar, by a count of dimensions in
$\bigwedge^2 K^4$. It states the agreement in the form that the
algebraic induction applies: a linear form on $\bigwedge^2 K^4$ that
vanishes on the meet vanishes on the join.

The proof finds a line of $\bigwedge^2 K^4$ that contains both
extensors, cut out by the coordinate pairing. The first lemma shows that
the meet lies on it.

\begin{lemma}[The complement of a $2$-extensor is orthogonal to the $2$-extensors sharing a vector with it]
  \label{lem:complement-iso-decomposable-wedge-perp}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.complementIso_toDual_eq_zero_of_wedgeProd_eq_zero,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.wedgeProd_extensor_eq_zero_of_shared_vector,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.complementIso_toDual_extensor_eq_zero_of_shared_vector}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:meet-complement-iso, lem:complement-iso-toDual, def:join, def:extensor}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] Let $d \ge 1$ and $0 \le j \le d+1$, and let
      $X \in \bigwedge^{j} K^{d+1}$ and $Y \in \bigwedge^{d+1-j} K^{d+1}$
      with $X \vee Y = 0$. Then $\langle *X, Y \rangle = 0$.
    \item[(ii)] Let $n_1, n_2 \in K^4$ be linearly independent, and let
      $Y = n \vee c$ with $n \in \{n_1, n_2\}$ and $c \in K^4$. Then
      $(n_1 \vee n_2) \vee Y = 0$ and
      $\langle *(n_1 \vee n_2), Y \rangle = 0$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  For (i), \cref{lem:complement-iso-toDual} gives
  $\langle *X, Y \rangle = \operatorname{vol}(X \vee Y) =
  \operatorname{vol}(0) = 0$. For (ii), $(n_1 \vee n_2) \vee (n \vee c)$
  is the extensor of the family $n_1, n_2, n, c$ (\cref{def:join}). This
  family repeats $n$, so its extensor is zero (the remark after
  \cref{def:extensor}). The second claim follows by (i), with
  $X = n_1 \vee n_2$ and $j = 2$.
\end{proof}
\begin{fmlnote}
  The first pin is (i). The second and third pins are (ii) for
  $n = n_1$, without the hypothesis that $n_1$ and $n_2$ are linearly
  independent. The formal proof of
  \cref{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union} obtains the case
  $n = n_2$ from the first pin, as above.
\end{fmlnote}

Geometrically, the line in the proof below is the image of
$\bigwedge^2 W$ in $\bigwedge^2 K^4$. The next two lemmas give the facts
that identify it, which we use after the proof.

\begin{lemma}[The exterior square of a $2$-dimensional space is a line]
  \label{lem:complement-iso-line-one-dim}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.finrank_exteriorPower_two_eq_one,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exteriorPower_finrank_eq_one_proportional}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:join, def:extensor}
  Let $W$ be a $2$-dimensional vector space over $K$. Then
  $\dim \bigwedge^{2} W = \binom{2}{2} = 1$. Consequently, every element
  of $\bigwedge^{2} W$ is a scalar multiple of any nonzero one.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The exterior power $\bigwedge^{n} W$ has dimension
  $\binom{\dim W}{n}$, which is $1$ for $n = \dim W = 2$. A space of
  dimension $1$ is spanned by each of its nonzero elements.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The exterior square of a subspace embeds in $\bigwedge^{2} K^4$]
  \label{lem:complement-iso-exterior-map-injective}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.exteriorPower_map_subtype_injective}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:meet-complement-iso}
  Let $W$ be a subspace of $K^4$. The linear map
  $\bigwedge^{2} W \to \bigwedge^{2} K^4$ induced by the inclusion of $W$
  in $K^4$ is injective.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Extend a basis $w_1, \dots, w_m$ of $W$ to a basis of $K^4$. The map
  sends the basis of $\bigwedge^2 W$ formed by the $w_a \vee w_b$ with
  $a < b$ to part of the corresponding basis of $\bigwedge^2 K^4$, so it
  is injective.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Point-join $\leftrightarrow$ panel-meet duality; KT eq.~(6.45)]
  \label{lem:case-III-claim612-line-in-panel-union}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.complementIso_smul_eq_extensor_join,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensor_join_eq_zero_of_complementIso_eq_zero,
        CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.extensor_join_eq_zero_of_complementIso_eq_zero_dotProduct}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:complement-iso-decomposable-wedge-perp, lem:complement-iso-line-one-dim,
        lem:complement-iso-exterior-map-injective, def:meet-complement-iso, def:join, def:extensor}
  Let $n_1, n_2 \in K^4$ be linearly independent, and let
  $x, y \in K^4$ satisfy $x \cdot n_i = y \cdot n_i = 0$ for $i = 1, 2$.
  Then $x \vee y$ is a scalar multiple of $*(n_1 \vee n_2)$. In
  particular, every linear form $r$ on $\bigwedge^2 K^4$ with
  $r(*(n_1 \vee n_2)) = 0$ satisfies $r(x \vee y) = 0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Let $\Phi$ be the subspace of $\bigwedge^2 K^4$ spanned by the
  $2$-extensors $n_1 \vee v$ and $n_2 \vee v$ for $v \in K^4$, and let
  \[
    \Omega = \{Z \in \textstyle\bigwedge^2 K^4 :
      \langle Z, F \rangle = 0 \text{ for every } F \in \Phi\}.
  \]
  We show that $\Omega$ has dimension $1$ and contains both $x \vee y$
  and $*(n_1 \vee n_2)$.

  First, $\Phi$ has dimension $5$. For $n \neq 0$, the map
  $v \mapsto n \vee v$ has kernel $\operatorname{span}\{n\}$, since
  $n \vee v = 0$ exactly when $n$ and $v$ are linearly dependent (the
  remark after \cref{def:extensor}). So its image $n \vee K^4$ has
  dimension $3$. The images for $n_1$ and $n_2$ meet in the line spanned
  by $n_1 \vee n_2$. This line lies in both, as
  $n_1 \vee n_2 = n_2 \vee (-n_1)$. Conversely, let
  $n_1 \vee v = n_2 \vee w$. The join with $n_2$ on the left gives
  $n_2 \vee n_1 \vee v = n_2 \vee n_2 \vee w = 0$, so $n_2$, $n_1$ and
  $v$ are linearly dependent. As $n_1$ and $n_2$ are independent,
  $v = \alpha n_1 + \beta n_2$ for some $\alpha, \beta \in K$, and
  $n_1 \vee v = \beta\, (n_1 \vee n_2)$. Hence
  $\dim \Phi = 3 + 3 - 1 = 5$
  (\texttt{finrank\_\allowbreak{}sup\_\allowbreak{}range\_\allowbreak{}wedgeFixedLeft}).
  The coordinate pairing is nondegenerate on the $6$-dimensional space
  $\bigwedge^2 K^4$, so $\dim \Omega = 6 - 5 = 1$.

  Next, $x \vee y \in \Omega$. It suffices to show that
  $\langle x \vee y, n_i \vee v \rangle = 0$ for $i = 1, 2$ and
  $v \in K^4$. Identify $V$ with $V^*$ by the dot product. Then the
  coordinate pairing of two $2$-extensors is the determinant of
  \cref{def:meet-pairing-dual}
  (\texttt{exteriorPower\_\allowbreak{}basis\_\allowbreak{}toDual\_\allowbreak{}eq\_\allowbreak{}pairingDual\_\allowbreak{}comp\_\allowbreak{}map}):
  \[
    \langle x \vee y, n_i \vee v \rangle
    = \det \begin{pmatrix} x \cdot n_i & x \cdot v \\
                           y \cdot n_i & y \cdot v \end{pmatrix}.
  \]
  Its first column is zero by hypothesis, so it vanishes
  (\texttt{extensor\_\allowbreak{}toDual\_\allowbreak{}extensor\_\allowbreak{}eq\_\allowbreak{}zero\_\allowbreak{}of\_\allowbreak{}perp}).

  The meet lies in $\Omega$ as well: by
  \cref{lem:complement-iso-decomposable-wedge-perp}(ii), the pairing
  $\langle *(n_1 \vee n_2), n_i \vee v \rangle$ vanishes for $i = 1, 2$
  and $v \in K^4$.

  Finally, $n_1 \vee n_2 \neq 0$, as $n_1$ and $n_2$ are linearly
  independent, and so $*(n_1 \vee n_2) \neq 0$, as $*$ is an isomorphism.
  So $*(n_1 \vee n_2)$ spans $\Omega$, and
  $x \vee y = \lambda\, {*(n_1 \vee n_2)}$ for some $\lambda \in K$. For a
  linear form $r$ with $r(*(n_1 \vee n_2)) = 0$, it follows that
  $r(x \vee y) = \lambda\, r(*(n_1 \vee n_2)) = 0$.
\end{proof}
\begin{fmlnote}
  The first pin is the first statement, and the second and third are
  the second statement, with the orthogonality written through the
  coordinate pairing of $K^4$ and through the dot product. The formal
  proof of Case~III at $d = 3$
  (\cref{lem:case-III-candidate-dispatch-d3}) applies the third, in the
  step from a join to a line of the proof of \cref{lem:case-III-claim612}.
  The general case applies \cref{lem:meet-two-hyperplanes} instead.
\end{fmlnote}

The line $\Omega$ is the image of $\bigwedge^2 W$. The inclusion of $W$
in $K^4$ induces an injective map $\bigwedge^2 W \to \bigwedge^2 K^4$
(\cref{lem:complement-iso-exterior-map-injective}). Its image is
therefore a line, as $\bigwedge^2 W$ is one
(\cref{lem:complement-iso-line-one-dim}). It is spanned
by $x \vee y$ for any basis $x, y$ of $W$, and $x \vee y$ lies in
$\Omega$ by the proof. So $*(n_1 \vee n_2)$ is a nonzero extensor of
$W$, and the meet of the two planes is an extensor of their line of
intersection. For
lines in $3$-space this is White's theorem that the Plücker coordinates
of a subspace and its dual Plücker coordinates, computed from hyperplanes
that contain it, agree up to a common scalar after a reordering with
signs~\cite[\S2]{white1994}. By the formula for $*e_S$ after
\cref{lem:complement-iso-toDual}, the complement isomorphism performs the
reordering.

Conversely, a nonzero $2$-extensor determines the plane spanned by its
vectors. This is the case $j = 2$ of the proposition of Barnabei, Brini
and Rota recalled after \cref{def:join}, here for every $d$.

\begin{lemma}[Equal nonzero $2$-extensors span the same plane]
  \label{lem:decomposable-extensor-span-unique}
  \lean{CombinatorialRigidity.Molecular.span_range_eq_of_extensor_eq}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:extensor, def:join}
  Let $p_0, p_1, p_0', p_1' \in K^{d+1}$ with
  $p_0 \vee p_1 = p_0' \vee p_1' \neq 0$. Then
  $\operatorname{span}\{p_0, p_1\} = \operatorname{span}\{p_0', p_1'\}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Let $x \in K^{d+1}$. The join $x \vee p_0 \vee p_1$ depends on $p_0$
  and $p_1$ only through $p_0 \vee p_1$ (\cref{def:join}), so
  $x \vee p_0 \vee p_1 = x \vee p_0' \vee p_1'$. The vectors $p_0$ and
  $p_1$ are linearly independent, as their join is nonzero, so
  $x \vee p_0 \vee p_1 = 0$ exactly when
  $x \in \operatorname{span}\{p_0, p_1\}$ (the remark after
  \cref{def:extensor}). The same holds for $p_0'$ and $p_1'$. So the two
  spans have the same elements.
\end{proof}
