\section{Independence and rank of molecular graphs}
\label{sec:jacobs}

This chapter proves two results of Jackson and
Jord\'an~\cite{jacksonJordan2008} on squares of graphs. We cite their
paper as JJ, as we cite Katoh and Tanigawa's as KT. The first result is
a conjecture of Jacobs~\cite{jacobs1998}: the square $G^2$ of a graph
$G$ is independent in the three-dimensional generic rigidity matroid if
and only if $G^2$ satisfies JJ's three-dimensional Laman condition
(\cref{thm:jacobs}). JJ state it as their Conjecture~5.1. They note that
Jacobs states it as a result, but that his proof assumes an observation
for which no proof was known (JJ~\S5). JJ derive the conjecture (their
Theorem~5.4) from their Conjecture~2.2, the rank formula
\[
  r(G^2) \;=\; 3|V| - 6 - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)
\]
for graphs $G = (V, E)$ of minimum degree at least two. This formula is
KT Corollary~5.7 (\cref{cor:molecule-rank-formula}), so the conjecture
holds.

The second result is the degree-one rank formula, JJ's Lemma~4.2. It
concerns connected graphs with vertices of degree one, to which the rank
formula does not apply. For a tree $G$ it gives $r(G^2)$ explicitly
(\cref{thm:degree-one-rank-tree}). For any other connected graph $G$,
it gives $r(G^2)$ through the rank of the square of the core of $G$, its
maximal subgraph of minimum degree at least two
(\cref{thm:degree-one-rank}). JJ prove it to show that the rank
formula, applied to the core, determines $r(G^2)$ for every graph.

The hard direction of Jacobs' conjecture rests on JJ's counting bound
(their Theorem~5.3): if $G$ has minimum degree at least two and $G^2$
satisfies the Laman condition, then
$|E(G^2)| \le 3|V| - 6 - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$. By the rank
formula the right-hand side is $r(G^2)$, so $E(G^2)$ is independent.
\Cref{sec:jacobs-laman3} defines the Laman condition, and shows that if
$G^2$ satisfies it, then $G$ has maximum degree at most three.
\Cref{sec:jacobs-tight-partitions} studies the partitions of $V$ that
attain the deficiency. \Cref{sec:jacobs-counting} proves the counting
bound by counting the edges of $G^2$ against one of them.
\Cref{sec:jacobs-easy} proves the easy direction of the conjecture, for
every graph. \Cref{sec:jacobs-zero-extension} proves the
$0$-extension lemma in the form we use: adding a vertex joined to at
most three vertices raises the rank by the number of new edges. \Cref{sec:jacobs-theorem}
proves the conjecture, deleting vertices of degree one by this lemma
until the minimum degree is at least two. \Cref{sec:jacobs-degree-one}
proves the degree-one rank formula in the same way.

Throughout, $V$ is a finite set, and a graph on $V$ is a simple graph
with vertex set $V$. We write $r = r_3$ for the rank function of the
generic rigidity matroid $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$ (\cref{def:genericRank}). We
call a graph $H$ on $V$ independent when $E(H)$ is independent in
$\mathcal{R}_3(V)$, that is, row-independent at some placement of $V$ in
$\R^3$ (\cref{lem:genericRigidityMatroid-indep-iff}). For a partition
$P$ of $V$, let $d_G(P)$ be the number of edges of $G$ that join
different parts of $P$. The value of $P$ is
\[
  \operatorname{def}_{\tilde G}(P) \;=\; 6(|P| - 1) - 5\,d_G(P),
\]
and the deficiency $\operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$ is the largest value
over the partitions of $V$. This is \cref{def:D-deficiency} at
$D = 6$, for the multigraph $\tilde G = 5 \cdot G$.

\subsection{The three-dimensional Laman condition}
\label{sec:jacobs-laman3}

This subsection defines JJ's counting condition for graphs in three
dimensions (JJ~\S5) and proves its first consequence for squares (JJ
Lemma~5.2): if $G^2$ satisfies the condition, then every vertex of $G$
has degree at most three. Following JJ, we call a graph that satisfies
the condition Laman. The condition is the three-dimensional analogue of
$(2,3)$-sparsity (\cref{def:isSparse}). Unlike the planar Laman graphs
of \cref{def:isLaman}, it asks for no equality on the number of edges.

\begin{definition}[The three-dimensional Laman condition]
  \label{def:isLaman3}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsLaman3}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:edgesIn}
  A graph $H$ on $V$ is \emph{Laman} (in the three-dimensional counting
  sense of \cite[\S5]{jacksonJordan2008}) if for every $X \subseteq V$
  with $|X| \ge 3$,
  \[
    \bigl|\edgesIn[H]{X}\bigr| \;\le\; 3|X| - 6 .
  \]
\end{definition}

\begin{fmlnote}
  \label{fmlnote:isLaman3-guard}
  The condition is not the $(k,\ell)$-sparsity of \cref{def:isSparse}
  at $(k, \ell) = (3, 6)$. That predicate bounds every set $X$ with
  $\ell \le k|X|$, and at $(3, 6)$ these include the sets with
  $|X| = 2$. For such a set the bound $3|X| - 6 = 0$ fails as soon as
  $X$ spans an edge, so no graph with an edge is $(3,6)$-sparse. The
  Laman condition bounds only the sets with $|X| \ge 3$, and $K_2$, for
  instance, satisfies it. At $(2, 3)$ the two guards agree, as
  $3 \le 2|X|$ means $|X| \ge 2$, and the planar Laman graphs of
  \cref{def:isLaman} are defined through $(2,3)$-sparsity. So the
  condition is formalized as a predicate of its own, which bounds only
  the sets with $|X| \ge 3$.
\end{fmlnote}

\begin{lemma}[Monotonicity]
  \label{lem:isLaman3-mono}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsLaman3.mono_left}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:isLaman3}
  A subgraph of a Laman graph is Laman.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:edgesIn-mono}
  Let $H$ be a subgraph of a Laman graph $H'$. Every edge of $H$ inside
  a set $X \subseteq V$ is an edge of $H'$ inside $X$, so
  $\bigl|\edgesIn[H]{X}\bigr| \le \bigl|\edgesIn[H']{X}\bigr|
  \le 3|X| - 6$ whenever $|X| \ge 3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Edge count of a clique]
  \label{lem:clique-edgesIn-count}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsClique.ncard_edgesIn}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:edgesIn}
  Let $H$ be a graph on $V$, and let $X \subseteq V$ be a clique of $H$,
  that is, a set of pairwise adjacent vertices. Then
  $\bigl|\edgesIn[H]{X}\bigr| = \binom{|X|}{2}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The edges of $H$ inside $X$ are exactly the two-element subsets of
  $X$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Maximum degree three; JJ Lemma 5.2]
  \label{lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsLaman3.degree_le_three}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:isLaman3, def:square-graph}
  Let $G$ be a graph such that $G^2$ is Laman. Then every vertex of $G$
  has degree at most three.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:square-cliques, lem:clique-edgesIn-count}
  Let $v$ be a vertex of $G$. Its closed neighborhood $N_G[v]$ is a
  clique of $G^2$ on $d_G(v) + 1$ vertices (\cref{lem:square-cliques}).
  If $d_G(v) \ge 4$, five of these vertices form a clique of $G^2$,
  which spans $\binom{5}{2} = 10 > 3 \cdot 5 - 6$ edges of $G^2$
  (\cref{lem:clique-edgesIn-count}). Then $G^2$ is not Laman. So
  $d_G(v) \le 3$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Tight partitions}
\label{sec:jacobs-tight-partitions}

The counting bound of \cref{sec:jacobs-counting} is proved against a
partition of $V$ that attains the deficiency. This subsection proves
the facts about such partitions that the count uses. They come from JJ
Lemma~3.2, which JJ take from their paper on the partition of a graph
into its maximal subgraphs of deficiency zero. We prove its part~(a), a
consequence of its part~(b), and two consequences of part~(a) that JJ
draw in the proofs of their Theorems~3.4 and~5.3: two parts are joined
by at most one edge, and two nonadjacent vertices in distinct parts
have at most one common neighbor. Apart from the existence of these
partitions, all of this follows from one identity, which gives the
change in the value $\operatorname{def}_{\tilde G}(P)$ when several
parts of $P$ are merged into one (\cref{lem:partitionDef-merge}). The
arguments are the same for every $D$. So we state the lemmas for the
$D$-deficiency of \cref{def:D-deficiency} at every $D$, and each lemma
names the bound on $D$ that it needs. The chapter applies them at
$D = 6$.

\begin{lemma}[Tight partitions exist]
  \label{lem:exists-tight-partition}
  \lean{Graph.IsTightPartition, Graph.exists_isTightPartition}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ be a multigraph whose vertex set is finite and nonempty. Then
  some partition $P$ of $V(G)$ attains the deficiency, that is,
  $\operatorname{def}_{\tilde G}(P) = \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$.
  Following JJ~\S2, we call such a partition \emph{tight}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  A finite set has finitely many partitions, so the maximum that
  defines $\operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$ is attained.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Deficiency under coarsening]
  \label{lem:partitionDef-merge}
  \lean{Graph.crossingEdgesWithin, Graph.partitionDef_merge}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ be a multigraph, let $P$ be a partition of $V(G)$, and let
  $Q \subseteq P$ with $|Q| \ge 2$. Let $P'$ be the partition obtained
  from $P$ by merging the members of $Q$ into a single part, and let
  $e_G(Q)$ be the number of edges of $G$ that join distinct members of
  $Q$. Then
  \[
    \operatorname{def}_{\tilde G}(P') \;=\;
    \operatorname{def}_{\tilde G}(P)
      \;-\; \bigl( D\,(|Q| - 1) - (D-1)\,e_G(Q) \bigr).
  \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The partition $P'$ has $|Q| - 1$ fewer parts than $P$. An edge joins
  different parts of $P'$ exactly when it joins different parts of $P$
  and does not join two members of $Q$, so
  $d_G(P') = d_G(P) - e_G(Q)$. The identity follows from the definition
  of the value (\cref{def:D-deficiency}). Read from right to left, it
  gives the change in value when a part is split into several: if a
  part of $P'$ is split into the members of $Q$, the value rises by
  $D(|Q| - 1) - (D-1)\,e_G(Q)$.
\end{proof}

JJ state their Lemma~3.2(a), with $D = 6$, for the subgraph $H$ of $G$
induced by the union of the members of $Q$: as a partition of $V(H)$,
$Q$ has $\operatorname{def}_{\tilde H}(Q) \ge 0$. An edge of $H$ joins
two members of $Q$ exactly when it is an edge of $G$ that does, so this
is the following inequality.

\begin{lemma}[Subfamilies of a tight partition; JJ Lemma 3.2(a)]
  \label{lem:tight-partition-subfamily}
  \lean{Graph.IsTightPartition.subfamily_le}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:D-deficiency, lem:exists-tight-partition}
  Let $G$ be a multigraph, let $P$ be a tight partition of $V(G)$, and
  let $Q \subseteq P$ with $|Q| \ge 2$. Then
  \[
    (D-1)\,e_G(Q) \;\le\; D\,(|Q| - 1),
  \]
  where $e_G(Q)$ is the number of edges of $G$ that join distinct
  members of $Q$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:partitionDef-merge}
  Merging the members of $Q$ changes the value of $P$ by
  $(D-1)\,e_G(Q) - D(|Q| - 1)$ (\cref{lem:partitionDef-merge}). The
  merged partition has value at most $\operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$,
  which is the value of $P$ because $P$ is tight. So the change is at
  most zero.
\end{proof}

JJ's Lemma~3.2(b) says that every part of a tight partition induces a
subgraph of deficiency zero. This chapter needs only the following
consequence, which JJ draw in the proof of their Theorem~5.3, and we
prove it directly.

\begin{lemma}[Parts of a tight partition; a consequence of JJ Lemma 3.2(b)]
  \label{lem:tight-partition-parts}
  \lean{Graph.IsTightPartition.parts}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:D-deficiency}
  Let $D \ge 2$, let $G$ be a simple graph, and let $P$ be a tight
  partition of $V(G)$. Then every part of $P$ is a singleton or has at
  least three vertices. In the latter case every vertex of the part has
  at least two neighbors in the part.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:partitionDef-merge}
  Let $A \in P$ with $|A| \ge 2$, let $v \in A$, and let $m$ be the
  number of neighbors of $v$ in $A$. Splitting $v$ off $A$ as a part of
  its own raises the value of $P$ by $D - (D-1)\,m$
  (\cref{lem:partitionDef-merge}, read from right to left with
  $Q = \{A \setminus \{v\}, \{v\}\}$). As $P$ is tight, the value does
  not rise, so $D \le (D-1)\,m$. Since $D \ge 2$, this forces $m \ge 2$.
  So $A$ contains $v$ and at least two neighbors of $v$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Edge multiplicity between parts of a tight partition]
  \label{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-mult}
  \lean{Graph.IsTightPartition.crossingEdgesWithin_pair_le_one}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:D-deficiency}
  Let $D \ge 3$, let $G$ be a simple graph, and let $P$ be a tight
  partition of $V(G)$. Then two distinct parts of $P$ are joined by at
  most one edge of $G$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:tight-partition-subfamily}
  Let $A \ne B$ be parts of $P$. For $Q = \{A, B\}$,
  \cref{lem:tight-partition-subfamily} gives $(D-1)\,e_G(Q) \le D$. If
  $e_G(Q) \ge 2$, then $2(D-1) \le D$, that is, $D \le 2$, against
  $D \ge 3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The common neighbor of two vertices in distinct parts]
  \label{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}
  \lean{Graph.IsTightPartition.eq_of_common_nbr}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:D-deficiency}
  Let $D \ge 5$, let $G$ be a simple graph, and let $P$ be a tight
  partition of $V(G)$. Then two nonadjacent vertices in distinct parts
  of $P$ have at most one common neighbor.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:tight-partition-subfamily, lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-mult}
  Let $u$ and $w$ lie in distinct parts of $P$, and suppose that they
  have two common neighbors $v \ne v'$. The vertices $u, v, w, v'$ are
  distinct, as $u \ne w$ and adjacent vertices are distinct. So $uv$,
  $vw$, $wv'$ and $v'u$ are four distinct edges of $G$, and they form a
  closed walk.

  Let $Q$ be the set of parts of $P$ that meet $\{u, v, w, v'\}$, so
  that $2 \le |Q| \le 4$. The walk meets at least two parts, so it
  enters every member of $Q$ from another member at least once. These
  entries use distinct edges, each joining two members of $Q$, so
  $e_G(Q) \ge |Q|$. Now \cref{lem:tight-partition-subfamily} gives
  $(D-1)\,|Q| \le D(|Q| - 1)$, that is, $D \le |Q| \le 4$, a
  contradiction. When $|Q| = 2$, two of the four edges join the same two
  parts, which \cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-mult} excludes
  directly.
\end{proof}

\begin{fmlnote}
  \label{fmlnote:tight-partition-consumed-forms}
  As in \cref{fmlnote:partition-labeling}, a partition is a labeling of
  the vertices, and a tight partition is a labeling whose value is the
  deficiency. Merging the members of $Q$ gives all their vertices one of
  their labels. Four of the formal statements assume less than the
  lemmas above. \Cref{lem:exists-tight-partition} does not need $V(G)$
  nonempty: the empty set has one partition, with no parts and value
  $-D$. \Cref{lem:partitionDef-merge} needs only $Q$ nonempty, as for
  $|Q| = 1$ merging changes nothing and $e_G(Q) = 0$.
  \Cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-mult} holds for every multigraph,
  with parallel edges counted. \Cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}
  does not assume the two vertices nonadjacent, and its proof above does
  not use this.
\end{fmlnote}

\subsection{The counting bound}
\label{sec:jacobs-counting}

This subsection proves JJ Theorem~5.3: if $G$ has minimum degree at
least two and $G^2$ is Laman, then
$|E(G^2)| \le 3|V| - 6 - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$
(\cref{thm:laman-square-count}). We follow JJ's proof, which counts the
edges of $G^2$ part by part against a tight partition of $V$. Inside a
part $A$ with $|A| \ge 3$, the Laman condition bounds them by
$3|A| - 6$. The endpoints of an edge of $G^2$ that joins two parts and
is not an edge of $G$ have a unique common neighbor, and the edge is
counted at the part that contains it. A part $A$ with $|A| \ge 3$
receives $2\,d_G(A)$ of these edges (JJ eq.~(6)), where $d_G(A)$ is the
number of edges of $G$ with exactly one endpoint in $A$. A singleton
part $\{v\}$ receives $2\,d_G(v) - 3$ of them (JJ eqs.~(5) and~(7)).
Summing over the parts gives the bound.

For the rest of this subsection, let $G$ be a simple graph of minimum
degree at least two whose square is Laman, and let $P$ be a tight
partition of $V$ at $D = 6$ (\cref{lem:exists-tight-partition}). Then
every vertex of $G$ has degree two or three
(\cref{lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three}). Every part of $P$ is a singleton
or has at least three vertices, and in the latter case each of its
vertices has at least two neighbors in the part
(\cref{lem:tight-partition-parts}). As in \cref{def:D-deficiency},
$d_G(P)$ is the number of edges of $G$ that join distinct parts.

Let $uw$ be an edge of $G^2$ whose endpoints lie in distinct parts.
Following JJ, we call $uw$ a \emph{cross edge} if it is not an edge of
$G$. Then $u$ and $w$ are at distance two in $G$
(\cref{def:square-graph}), so they have a common neighbor $v$. It is
unique (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}), and we call it the
common neighbor of the cross edge. At least one of the edges $uv$ and
$vw$ joins two parts, as $u$ and $w$ lie in distinct parts. JJ call the
cross edge \emph{normal} if exactly one of them does, and
\emph{special} if both do. So $uw$ is normal if $v$ lies in the part of
$u$ or in that of $w$, and special otherwise.

\begin{lemma}[Classification of the edges of the square]
  \label{lem:square-cross-classification}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.square_edgeSet_eq_union, SimpleGraph.disjoint_inPart_gCross,
    SimpleGraph.disjoint_inPart_cross, SimpleGraph.disjoint_gCross_cross,
    SimpleGraph.squareCrossEdges_eq_union,
    SimpleGraph.squareNormalCrossEdges_disjoint_special,
    SimpleGraph.squareGCrossEdges_ncard_eq_crossingEdges,
    SimpleGraph.squareSpecialCrossEdges_singleton_part,
    SimpleGraph.squareNormalCrossEdges_part_three_le}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above. Then $E(G^2)$ is the disjoint union of
  four classes: the edges inside a part, the $d_G(P)$ edges of $G$ that
  join distinct parts, the normal cross edges, and the special cross
  edges. Moreover, the common neighbor of a special cross edge forms a
  singleton part. The common neighbor of a normal cross edge lies in a
  part of at least three vertices, together with exactly one endpoint of
  the edge.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr, lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three,
    lem:tight-partition-parts}
  An edge of $G^2$ lies inside a part or joins two parts. In the latter
  case it is an edge of $G$ or a cross edge, and a cross edge is normal
  or special. The first three classes are disjoint by definition. The
  last two are disjoint because the common neighbor of a cross edge is
  unique (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}).

  Let $uw$ be a special cross edge with common neighbor $v$. Then $u$ and
  $w$ are two neighbors of $v$ outside its part. If the part of $v$ were
  not a singleton, $v$ would also have two neighbors inside it
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-parts}), and so degree at least four. This
  contradicts the degree bound (\cref{lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three}).
  Now let $uw$ be a normal cross edge with common neighbor $v$. As $u$
  and $w$ lie in distinct parts, $v$ shares its part with exactly one of
  them. This part contains two adjacent vertices, so it is not a
  singleton, and it has at least three vertices
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-parts}).
\end{proof}

We first count the special cross edges.

\begin{lemma}[Special cross edges at a singleton part; JJ eq.\ (5), (7)]
  \label{lem:singleton-part-neighborhood}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.isClique_neighborSet_square,
    SimpleGraph.ncard_edgesIn_neighborSet_square,
    SimpleGraph.IsSquareTightPartition.mem_squareSpecialCrossEdges_of_singleton_part}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above, and let $\{v\}$ be a singleton part of
  $P$. Then the neighborhood $N_G(v)$ is a clique of $G^2$, all of whose
  edges are special cross edges with common neighbor $v$, and
  \[
    \bigl|\edgesIn[G^2]{N_G(v)}\bigr| \;=\; 2\,d_G(v) - 3 .
  \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:square-cliques, lem:clique-edgesIn-count,
    lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three, lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-mult,
    lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr, lem:tight-partition-subfamily}
  The neighborhood $N_G(v)$ is a clique of $G^2$
  (\cref{lem:square-cliques}). Let $u \ne w$ be neighbors of $v$.
  Neither lies in the part $\{v\}$. They do not lie in one part $A$, as
  then $vu$ and $vw$ would be two edges between $A$ and $\{v\}$
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-mult}). They are not adjacent in
  $G$. Otherwise the triangle $uvw$ would put three edges between the
  three parts that contain $u$, $v$ and $w$. Then $3(D - 1) \le 2D$
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-subfamily}), that is, $D \le 3$, while
  $D = 6$. So $uw$ is a cross edge with common neighbor $v$. It is
  special, as the part $\{v\}$ contains neither $u$ nor $w$.

  Finally, $v$ has degree two or three
  (\cref{lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three}). So the clique has
  $\binom{d_G(v)}{2} = 2\,d_G(v) - 3$ edges
  (\cref{lem:clique-edgesIn-count}).
\end{proof}

JJ also state the converse.

\begin{lemma}[Special cross edges arise from a unique singleton part]
  \label{lem:singleton-part-converse}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.exists_unique_singleton_part_of_mem_squareSpecialCrossEdges}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above. Then every special cross edge is an edge
  of $G^2$ inside $N_G(v)$ for exactly one singleton part $\{v\}$ of
  $P$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:singleton-part-neighborhood, lem:square-cross-classification,
    lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}
  Let $uw$ be a special cross edge with common neighbor $v$. Then
  $\{v\}$ is a part of $P$ (\cref{lem:square-cross-classification}), and
  $u, w \in N_G(v)$. If also $u, w \in N_G(v')$ for a singleton part
  $\{v'\}$, then $v'$ is a common neighbor of $u$ and $w$, so $v' = v$
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}).
\end{proof}

We now count the normal cross edges, following JJ's proof of their
eq.~(6). Let $uw$ be a normal cross edge with common neighbor $v$.
Exactly one of the edges $uv$ and $vw$ lies inside a part, and JJ root
$uw$ at that part, which is the part of $v$.

\begin{lemma}[The root of a normal cross edge]
  \label{lem:normal-cross-count-rooted}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.squareNormalCrossEdgesRootedAt,
    SimpleGraph.IsSquareTightPartition.rootedAt_inj}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above. We say that a normal cross edge is
  \emph{rooted} at the part of $P$ that contains its common neighbor.
  Then every normal cross edge is rooted at exactly one part, and this
  part has at least three vertices.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:square-cross-classification, lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}
  The common neighbor of a normal cross edge is unique
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}), so the edge is rooted at
  exactly one part. This part has at least three vertices by
  \cref{lem:square-cross-classification}.
\end{proof}

Let $A$ be a part with $|A| \ge 3$. We show in the next three lemmas
that each edge $vw$ of $G$ with $v \in A$ and $w \notin A$ gives exactly
two normal cross edges rooted at $A$: the edges $uw$, for the two
neighbors $u$ of $v$ in $A$.

\begin{lemma}[Two neighbors in the part]
  \label{lem:normal-cross-count-local}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsSquareTightPartition.ncard_inPartNeighbors_eq_two}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above, let $A$ be a part of $P$ with
  $|A| \ge 3$, and let $v \in A$ have a neighbor outside $A$. Then $v$
  has exactly two neighbors in $A$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three, lem:tight-partition-parts}
  By \cref{lem:tight-partition-parts}, $v$ has at least two neighbors in
  $A$. It has at most three neighbors
  (\cref{lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three}), and one of them lies outside
  $A$. So exactly two lie in $A$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[A normal cross edge from two edges at a vertex]
  \label{lem:normal-cross-count-producer}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsSquareTightPartition.mk_mem_squareNormalCrossEdgesRootedAt}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above, let $v$ be a vertex of $G$, and let $u$
  and $w$ be neighbors of $v$ with $u$ in the part of $v$ and $w$
  outside it. Then $uw$ is a normal cross edge rooted at the part of
  $v$, and $v$ is the only common neighbor of $u$ and $w$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:square-cliques, lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-mult,
    lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}
  The vertices $u$ and $w$ lie in distinct parts, and both are neighbors
  of $v$. So $uw$ is an edge of $G^2$ (\cref{lem:square-cliques}) that
  joins two parts. It is not an edge of $G$. Otherwise $vw$ and $uw$
  would be two edges between the part of $v$ and that of $w$
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-mult}). So $uw$ is a cross edge, and $v$ is its common neighbor
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}). As $v$ lies in the part
  of $u$, the cross edge is normal and rooted at the part of $v$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Two normal cross edges for each edge leaving a part]
  \label{lem:normal-cross-count-fiber}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsSquareTightPartition.ncard_normalCrossEdges_of_crossing_eq_two}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above, let $A$ be a part of $P$ with
  $|A| \ge 3$, and let $vw$ be an edge of $G$ with $v \in A$ and
  $w \notin A$. Then the edges $uw$ with $u \in N_G(v) \cap A$, which
  are normal cross edges rooted at $A$
  (\cref{lem:normal-cross-count-producer}), number exactly two.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:normal-cross-count-producer, lem:normal-cross-count-local}
  The map $u \mapsto uw$ is injective on $N_G(v) \cap A$, as no vertex of
  $A$ is $w$. So there are as many edges $uw$ as neighbors of $v$ in $A$,
  that is, two (\cref{lem:normal-cross-count-local}). Each is a normal
  cross edge rooted at $A$ (\cref{lem:normal-cross-count-producer}).
\end{proof}

It remains to show that every normal cross edge rooted at $A$ arises in
this way, and from only one edge of $G$. We count these edges of $G$ by
the degree of $A$, in the sense of JJ~\S3.

\begin{definition}[The degree of a part]
  \label{def:g-cut-edges}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.gCutEdges}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:D-deficiency}
  Let $P$ be a partition of $V$, and let $A$ be a part of $P$. We write
  $d_G(A)$ for the number of edges $xy$ of $G$ with $x \in A$ and
  $y \notin A$, that is, with exactly one endpoint in $A$.
\end{definition}

For a singleton part $\{v\}$, every edge at $v$ has exactly one endpoint
in $\{v\}$, so $d_G(\{v\})$ is the degree $d_G(v)$ of $v$.

\begin{lemma}[The normal cross edges rooted at a part, by the edges leaving it]
  \label{lem:normal-cross-count-partition}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.squareCutPairs, SimpleGraph.ncard_squareCutPairs_eq_gCutEdges,
    SimpleGraph.IsSquareTightPartition.squareNormalCrossEdgesRootedAt_eq_biUnion,
    SimpleGraph.IsSquareTightPartition.squareCutPairs_pairwiseDisjoint}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency, def:g-cut-edges}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above, and let $A$ be a part of $P$. Then
  $(v, w) \mapsto vw$ is a bijection from the pairs $(v, w)$ with
  $v \in A$, $w \notin A$ and $vw \in E(G)$ onto the $d_G(A)$ edges of
  $G$ with exactly one endpoint in $A$. The normal cross edges rooted at
  $A$ are the union, over these pairs $(v, w)$, of the sets
  $\{uw : u \in N_G(v) \cap A\}$, and the sets of two distinct pairs are
  disjoint.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:normal-cross-count-producer, lem:square-cross-classification,
    lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}
  An edge of $G$ with exactly one endpoint in $A$ is $vw$ for exactly one
  of these pairs, the one with $v \in A$. By
  \cref{lem:normal-cross-count-producer}, every edge $uw$ with
  $u \in N_G(v) \cap A$ is a normal cross edge rooted at $A$. Conversely,
  let $e$ be a normal cross edge rooted at $A$, with common neighbor
  $v \in A$. Exactly one endpoint $u$ of $e$ lies in $A$
  (\cref{lem:square-cross-classification}). Let $w$ be the other. Then
  $(v, w)$ is one of the pairs, and $e = uw$ with $u \in N_G(v) \cap A$.

  Finally, let $uw$, with $u \in A$, lie in the sets of two pairs
  $(v, w)$ and $(v', w')$. Then $w' = w$, as $w$ is the endpoint outside
  $A$. So $v$ and $v'$ are common neighbors of $u$ and $w$, and $v = v'$
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Normal cross edges rooted at a part; JJ eq.\ (6)]
  \label{lem:normal-cross-count}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsSquareTightPartition.ncard_normalCrossEdgesRootedAt_eq_two_mul_gCutEdges}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency, def:g-cut-edges}
  Let $G$ and $P$ be as above, and let $A$ be a part of $P$ with
  $|A| \ge 3$. Then the normal cross edges rooted at $A$
  (\cref{lem:normal-cross-count-rooted}) number exactly $2\,d_G(A)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:normal-cross-count-partition, lem:normal-cross-count-fiber}
  By \cref{lem:normal-cross-count-partition}, the normal cross edges
  rooted at $A$ are the disjoint union of $d_G(A)$ sets, one for each
  edge of $G$ with exactly one endpoint in $A$. Each of these sets has
  exactly two elements (\cref{lem:normal-cross-count-fiber}).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The counting bound for Laman squares; JJ Theorem 5.3]
  \label{thm:laman-square-count}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.laman_square_count}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:isLaman3, def:square-graph, def:D-deficiency}
  Let $G$ be a simple graph of minimum degree at least two on a finite
  nonempty vertex set $V$, and suppose $G^2$ is Laman. Then
  \[
    |E(G^2)| \;\le\; 3|V| - 6 - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G).
  \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:exists-tight-partition, lem:square-cross-classification,
    lem:singleton-part-neighborhood, lem:singleton-part-converse,
    lem:normal-cross-count, lem:tight-partition-parts}
  Let $P$ be a tight partition of $V$
  (\cref{lem:exists-tight-partition}), and let $t$ be its number of
  parts. Let $C_n$ and $C_s$ be the numbers of normal and of special
  cross edges. By \cref{lem:square-cross-classification},
  \[
    |E(G^2)| \;=\; \sum_{A \in P} \bigl|\edgesIn[G^2]{A}\bigr|
      + d_G(P) + C_n + C_s .
  \]
  Each part has one vertex or at least three
  (\cref{lem:tight-partition-parts}). A singleton part spans no edge,
  and a part $A$ with $|A| \ge 3$ spans at most $3|A| - 6$ edges of
  $G^2$, as $G^2$ is Laman.

  We count each cross edge at the part that contains its common
  neighbor, which is unique (\cref{lem:tight-partition-cross-pair-nbr}).
  The normal cross edges counted at a part $A$ are those rooted at $A$,
  and there are none unless $|A| \ge 3$
  (\cref{lem:normal-cross-count-rooted}). So $C_n$ is the sum of
  $2\,d_G(A)$ over the parts $A$ with $|A| \ge 3$
  (\cref{lem:normal-cross-count}), which is JJ eq.~(6).

  The special cross edges are counted at singleton parts
  (\cref{lem:square-cross-classification}). Those counted at $\{v\}$
  have common neighbor $v$, so they lie inside $N_G(v)$. Conversely, the
  edges of $G^2$ inside $N_G(v)$ are special cross edges with common
  neighbor $v$, and there are $2\,d_G(v) - 3$ of them
  (\cref{lem:singleton-part-neighborhood}). So $C_s$ is the sum of
  $2\,d_G(v) - 3$ over the singleton parts $\{v\}$, which is JJ
  eqs.~(5) and~(7).

  For a singleton part $A = \{v\}$ we have $d_G(A) = d_G(v)$, so
  $2\,d_G(v) - 3 = (3|A| - 6) + 2\,d_G(A)$. Hence, for each part $A$,
  the edges of $G^2$ inside $A$ and the cross edges counted at $A$
  number at most $3|A| - 6 + 2\,d_G(A)$, and
  \[
    |E(G^2)| \;\le\; \sum_{A \in P} \bigl(3|A| - 6 + 2\,d_G(A)\bigr)
      + d_G(P) \;=\; 3|V| - 6t + 5\,d_G(P).
  \]
  Here $\sum_A |A| = |V|$, and $\sum_A d_G(A) = 2\,d_G(P)$, as an edge
  that joins two parts has one endpoint in each. The right-hand side is
  $3|V| - 6 - \operatorname{def}_{\tilde G}(P)$
  (\cref{def:D-deficiency}, at $D = 6$), and
  $\operatorname{def}_{\tilde G}(P) = \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)$, as
  $P$ is tight.
\end{proof}

\begin{fmlnote}
  \label{fmlnote:normal-cross-split}
  Formally, the classes of \cref{lem:square-cross-classification} are
  defined for every partition, and a cross edge is normal or special
  according to some common neighbor of its endpoints. These two classes
  are disjoint because the partition is tight, which makes the common
  neighbor unique. The formal statements of this subsection assume of
  $G$ and $P$ only what their proofs use. So only those whose proofs use
  the degree bound assume that $G^2$ is Laman, and among the lemmas only
  the count of \cref{lem:singleton-part-neighborhood} assumes the
  minimum degree, at $v$. The theorem does not assume $V$ nonempty: for
  $V = \emptyset$ both sides of its bound are $0$, as the empty
  partition has value $-6$. The last claim of
  \cref{lem:normal-cross-count-rooted} is one of the formal statements of
  \cref{lem:square-cross-classification}, and the bijection of
  \cref{lem:normal-cross-count-partition} is stated formally as the
  equality of the two counts.
\end{fmlnote}

\subsection{Independence implies the Laman condition}
\label{sec:jacobs-easy}

This subsection proves the easy direction of Jacobs' conjecture: if
$E(H)$ is independent in $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$, then $H$ is Laman
(\cref{cor:genericMatroid-indep-isLaman3}). It holds for every graph $H$
on $V$, square or not. JJ (\S5) derive it from their eq.~(1), the bound
$\operatorname{rank} R(H, p) \le 3|V| - 6$ for frameworks on at least
three vertices, applied to the subgraph induced on each set of at least
three vertices. Our proof is that of the plane
(\cref{lem:isSparse-of-rowIndependent-two}), one dimension up. The rank
bound we use needs the points to affinely span the space
(\cref{lem:rigidityMap-finrank-range-le-of-affinelySpanning}). So we
first prove the count at a placement at which every set of at least four
points affinely spans $\R^3$, and then choose such a placement that is
also generic.

\begin{lemma}[The Laman condition from row independence, dimension three]
  \label{lem:isLaman3-of-rowIndependent}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.isLaman3_of_edgeSetRowIndependent_dim_three}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:isLaman3, def:edgeSet-rowIndependent}
  Let $p$ be a placement of $V$ in $\R^3$ such that, for every set $S$
  of at least four vertices, the points $p(v)$, $v \in S$, affinely
  span $\R^3$. Let $H$ be a graph on $V$ whose edge set is
  row-independent at $p$. Then $H$ is Laman.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:rigidityMap-finrank-range-le-of-affinelySpanning}
  Let $X \subseteq V$ with $|X| \ge 3$. If $|X| = 3$, then $X$ spans at
  most $3 = 3|X| - 6$ edges. Let $|X| \ge 4$, let $H[X]$ be the subgraph
  of $H$ induced on $X$, and let $p|_X$ be the restriction of $p$ to
  $X$. The row of an edge of $H[X]$ at $p|_X$, composed with the
  restriction $(\R^3)^V \to (\R^3)^X$, is the row of $H$ at $p$ indexed
  by the same edge. The rows of $H$ at $p$ are linearly independent, so
  the rows of $H[X]$ at $p|_X$ are too, and their number is the rank of
  $R(H[X], p|_X)$, as in the proof of
  \cref{lem:isSparse-of-rowIndependent-two}:
  \[
    \bigl|\edgesIn[H]{X}\bigr| \;=\; \operatorname{rank} R(H[X], p|_X).
  \]
  The points $p(v)$, $v \in X$, affinely span $\R^3$, so this rank is at
  most $3|X| - 6$
  (\cref{lem:rigidityMap-finrank-range-le-of-affinelySpanning}, at
  $d = 3$).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Independent edge sets are Laman]
  \label{cor:genericMatroid-indep-isLaman3}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.isLaman3_of_genericRigidityMatroid_indep}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:isLaman3, def:genericRigidityMatroid}
  Let $H$ be a graph on $V$ with $E(H)$ independent in
  $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$. Then $H$ is Laman.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:genericRigidityMatroid-indep-iff,
    lem:isLaman3-of-rowIndependent, lem:exists-generic-general-position,
    def:general-position-placement}
  Choose a placement $p$ of $V$ in $\R^3$ that is generic for row
  independence and in general position up to order four
  (\cref{lem:exists-generic-general-position}). As $E(H)$ is
  independent, it is row-independent at some placement
  (\cref{lem:genericRigidityMatroid-indep-iff}), and hence at $p$, since
  $p$ is generic. A set of at least four vertices contains four whose
  points are affinely independent
  (\cref{def:general-position-placement}), and four affinely independent
  points affinely span $\R^3$. So \cref{lem:isLaman3-of-rowIndependent}
  applies at $p$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Zero-extensions in dimension three}
\label{sec:jacobs-zero-extension}

The reduction of Jacobs' conjecture to graphs of minimum degree at least
two (\cref{sec:jacobs-theorem}) and the degree-one rank formula
(\cref{sec:jacobs-degree-one}) both use the $0$-extension lemma. JJ
state it as their Lemma~3.3, citing Whiteley's Vertex 3-Addition
Lemma~9.1.3~\cite{whiteley1996}: if a graph is obtained from $H$ by
adding a new vertex joined to $s \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ distinct vertices of
$H$, then it is independent if and only if $H$ is. They call this
operation a \emph{$0$-extension}. Whiteley's lemma is stated at a fixed
placement, for a new vertex joined to three vertices: if the point of
the new vertex and the points of its three neighbors are not coplanar,
then the new framework is independent if and only if the old one is, and
its rank is larger by three.

Since $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$ is defined on a fixed vertex set, we keep $V$
fixed. For a vertex $v$ of a graph $H$ on $V$, let $E_H(v)$ be the
set of edges of $H$ at $v$, and let $H - E_H(v)$ be the graph obtained
from $H$ by deleting them. Then $v$ is isolated in $H - E_H(v)$, and if
$1 \le d_H(v) \le 3$, then $H$ is obtained from $H - E_H(v)$ by a
$0$-extension at $v$. We prove the lemma at a fixed placement
(\cref{lem:zero-extension-rowIndependent}) and derive two corollaries
for the generic rank. If $d_H(v) \le 3$, then the rank of $H$ exceeds
that of $H - E_H(v)$ by $d_H(v)$, and $H$ is independent if and only if
$H - E_H(v)$ is (\cref{cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three}). At a vertex
of any degree, the rank grows by $\min(3, d_H(v))$ if the neighborhood
$N_H(v)$ is a clique of $H$ (\cref{cor:zero-extension-clique-rank}).

The clique hypothesis cannot be dropped. The star $K_{1,4}$ with center
$v$ has rank four, not $0 + \min(3, 4) = 3$: at a generic placement,
each of its four rows is the only row with nonzero entries in the
columns of its leaf. JJ invoke the same hypothesis in the proof of their
Theorem~4.3, where they apply their Lemma~3.3 at vertices whose degree
may exceed three: ``the neighbour sets \dots\ induce complete (and hence
rigid) subgraphs''. Both applications below, the degree-one rank
formulas of \cref{sec:jacobs-degree-one}, satisfy it. There the vertex
removed has degree one in $G$, and its neighbors in $G^2$ lie in a
closed neighborhood of $G$, which is a clique of $G^2$
(\cref{lem:square-leaf-neighborhood}).

The subsection ends with the restriction of the generic rigidity matroid
to a subset of the vertices, and with four facts about squares and
induced subgraphs. The reduction of Jacobs' conjecture uses them all.

\begin{lemma}[Row-independence lift for a vertex of degree at most three]
  \label{lem:zero-extension-rowIndependent}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.zero_extension_edgeSetRowIndependent_lift}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:edgeSet-rowIndependent}
  Let $H$ be a graph on $V$, let $v$ be a vertex of $H$ of degree
  $s \le 3$ with neighbors $u_1, \dots, u_s$, and let
  $H' = H - E_H(v)$. Suppose that $E(H')$ is row-independent at a
  placement $p'$ of $V$ in $\R^3$ at which the points
  $p'(u_1), \dots, p'(u_s)$ are affinely independent. Then there is a
  placement $p$ that agrees with $p'$ away from $v$ and at which $E(H)$
  is row-independent.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  The points $p'(u_1), \dots, p'(u_s)$ are affinely independent and
  $s \le 3$, so their affine hull is a proper affine subspace of $\R^3$.
  Choose a point $q$ outside it, and let $p$ place $v$ at $q$ and agree
  with $p'$ elsewhere. The vectors $q - p'(u_i)$ are linearly
  independent. Indeed, let $\sum_i c_i \bigl(q - p'(u_i)\bigr) = 0$. If
  $\sum_i c_i \ne 0$, then $q$ is an affine combination of the points
  $p'(u_i)$, which it is not. So $\sum_i c_i = 0$, the relation reads
  $\sum_i c_i\, p'(u_i) = 0$, and every $c_i$ is zero by affine
  independence.

  The edges of $H$ are the edges of $H'$ and the edges $vu_i$. Neither
  end of an edge of $H'$ is $v$, so its row at $p$ is its row at $p'$
  (\cref{lem:rigidityMap-apply}). Suppose that a linear combination of
  the rows of $E(H)$ at $p$ vanishes, with coefficient $c_i$ at the row
  of $vu_i$. For $\alpha \in \R^3$, let $x^{\alpha}$ give $v$ the
  velocity $\alpha$ and every other vertex the velocity zero, as in the
  proof of \cref{lem:typeI-rowIndependent-extend}. The rows of the edges
  of $H'$ vanish at $x^{\alpha}$, and the row of $vu_i$ takes the value
  $\langle q - p'(u_i),\, \alpha \rangle$. Evaluating the relation at
  every $x^{\alpha}$ gives $\sum_i c_i \bigl(q - p'(u_i)\bigr) = 0$, so
  every $c_i$ is zero. What remains is a vanishing combination of the
  rows of $E(H')$ at $p'$, and its coefficients are zero, as $E(H')$ is
  row-independent at $p'$.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis that $p'(u_1), \dots, p'(u_s)$ are affinely independent
cannot be dropped either. On the six vertices $v, u_1, u_2, a, b, c$,
let $H$ consist of the complete graph on $u_1, u_2, a, b, c$ minus the
edge $u_1u_2$, together with the edges $vu_1$ and $vu_2$, and let
$H' = H - E_H(v)$. Let $p'$ place $a$, $b$ and $c$ at three
non-collinear points, and both $u_1$ and $u_2$ at one point off their
plane. Then $E(H')$ is row-independent at $p'$, but $E(H)$ is
row-dependent at every placement that agrees with $p'$ away from $v$.

Indeed, the rows of the three edges of $H'$ at $u_1$ are the only ones
with nonzero entries in the columns of $u_1$, and there their entries
$p'(u_1) - p'(x)$, $x \in \{a, b, c\}$, are linearly independent. The
same holds at $u_2$, and the rows of the triangle $abc$ are independent.
So the nine rows of $E(H')$ are independent, and the infinitesimal
motions of $H'$ at $p'$, restricted to $u_1, u_2, a, b, c$, form a space
of dimension $15 - 9 = 6$. They are the trivial motions, as these five
points affinely span $\R^3$. At a placement that agrees with $p'$ away
from $v$, the difference of the rows of $vu_1$ and $vu_2$ involves only
the velocities of $u_1$ and $u_2$. It vanishes on the trivial motions,
which give $u_1$ and $u_2$ the same velocity. So it vanishes on every
infinitesimal motion of $H'$ at $p'$, and it lies in the span of the
rows of $E(H')$. The corollaries below meet the hypothesis by taking
$p'$ generic for row independence and in general position up to order
four (\cref{lem:exists-generic-general-position}).

\begin{corollary}[$0$-extension at a vertex of degree at most three; JJ Lemma 3.3]
  \label{cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.zero_extension_genericRank_add_degree,
    SimpleGraph.zero_extension_indep_iff_of_degree_le_three}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:genericRank, def:genericRigidityMatroid}
  Let $H$ be a graph on $V$ and let $v$ be a vertex of $H$ with
  $d_H(v) \le 3$. Then
  \[
    r(H) \;=\; r\bigl(H - E_H(v)\bigr) + d_H(v),
  \]
  and $E(H)$ is independent in $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$ if and only if
  $E(H - E_H(v))$ is.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:zero-extension-rowIndependent,
    lem:genericRigidityMatroid-indep-iff,
    lem:exists-generic-general-position, def:general-position-placement}
  Let $H' = H - E_H(v)$ and $s = d_H(v)$. The edges of $H$ are the
  edges of $H'$ and the $s$ edges at $v$. Adding $s$ elements to a set
  raises its rank by at most $s$, so $r(H) \le r(H') + s$.

  For the reverse inequality, choose a placement $p'$ of $V$ in $\R^3$
  that is generic for row independence and in general position up to
  order four (\cref{lem:exists-generic-general-position}). Let $J$ be a
  maximal independent subset of $E(H')$, so that $|J| = r(H')$, and let
  $H_J$ be the graph whose edges are those of $J$ and the $s$ edges at
  $v$. Then $H_J - E_{H_J}(v)$ has edge set $J$. As $p'$ is generic,
  $J$ is row-independent at $p'$
  (\cref{lem:genericRigidityMatroid-indep-iff}). The neighbors of $v$ in
  $H_J$ are its $s \le 3$ neighbors in $H$, and their points are
  affinely independent, as $p'$ is in general position
  (\cref{def:general-position-placement}). So $E(H_J)$ is
  row-independent at some placement
  (\cref{lem:zero-extension-rowIndependent}), that is, independent in
  $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$. It is a subset of $E(H)$ with $r(H') + s$
  elements, so $r(H) \ge r(H') + s$.

  Finally, $|E(H)| = |E(H')| + s$, and a finite edge set is independent
  exactly when its rank equals its size. So $E(H)$ is independent if and
  only if $E(H')$ is.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Rank of a $0$-extension at a clique neighborhood]
  \label{cor:zero-extension-clique-rank}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.zero_extension_genericRank_add_min_le,
    SimpleGraph.zero_extension_genericRank_add_min_of_isClique}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:genericRank}
  Let $H$ be a graph on $V$ and let $v$ be a vertex of $H$ whose
  neighborhood $N_H(v)$ is a clique of $H$. Then
  \[
    r(H) \;=\; r\bigl(H - E_H(v)\bigr) + \min\bigl(3,\, d_H(v)\bigr).
  \]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three,
    cor:genericMatroid-indep-isLaman3}
  Let $H' = H - E_H(v)$ and $s = d_H(v)$. If $s \le 3$, the claim is
  \cref{cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three}. Let $s \ge 4$, fix three
  neighbors $u_1, u_2, u_3$ of $v$, and let $H_3$ be $H'$ together with
  the edges $vu_1$, $vu_2$ and $vu_3$. Then $H_3 - E_{H_3}(v) = H'$ and
  $d_{H_3}(v) = 3$, so $r(H_3) = r(H') + 3$
  (\cref{cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three}). As
  $E(H_3) \subseteq E(H)$, this gives $r(H) \ge r(H') + 3$. This lower
  bound holds without the clique hypothesis.

  For the upper bound we show that every further edge $vw$, with
  $w \in N_H(v) \setminus \{u_1, u_2, u_3\}$, lies in the closure of
  $E(H_3)$ in $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$. Then $E(H)$ lies in this closure, and
  $r(H) \le r(H_3) = r(H') + 3$. As $N_H(v)$ is a clique, the five
  vertices $v, u_1, u_2, u_3, w$ span a complete graph $K_5$ in $H$. Its
  edges other than $vw$ lie in $E(H_3)$: the edges $vu_i$ by
  construction, and the six edges among $u_1, u_2, u_3, w$ because they
  are edges of $H$ not at $v$.

  These nine edges are independent. Indeed, starting from the graph with
  no edges, join $u_2$ to $u_1$, then $u_3$ to $u_1$ and $u_2$, then $w$
  to $u_1$, $u_2$ and $u_3$, and last $v$ to $u_1$, $u_2$ and $u_3$.
  Each step is a $0$-extension at a vertex of degree at most three, which
  preserves independence (\cref{cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three}).
  All ten edges of the $K_5$ are dependent. Otherwise the graph they
  form would be Laman (\cref{cor:genericMatroid-indep-isLaman3}). But
  its five vertices form a clique, which spans
  $\binom{5}{2} = 10 > 3 \cdot 5 - 6$ edges
  (\cref{lem:clique-edgesIn-count}). Hence $vw$ lies in the closure of
  the other nine edges, and so in the closure of $E(H_3)$.
\end{proof}

The reduction of Jacobs' conjecture restricts a graph to its
non-isolated vertices, and so changes the vertex set of the matroid. The
next lemma compares the generic rigidity matroids of a set and of a
subset. Its proof is the same in every dimension.

\begin{lemma}[Independence and rank under vertex-set restriction]
  \label{lem:genericMatroid-induce-transport}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.genericRigidityMatroid_indep_image_iff,
    SimpleGraph.genericRank_eq_rk_image}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:genericRigidityMatroid, def:genericRank}
  Let $d \in \N$, let $S \subseteq V$, and let $H$ be a graph on $S$.
  Then $E(H)$ is independent in $\mathcal{R}_d(S)$ if and only if its
  image under the inclusion $S \hookrightarrow V$ is independent in
  $\mathcal{R}_d(V)$. The rank of $E(H)$ in $\mathcal{R}_d(S)$ equals
  the rank of its image in $\mathcal{R}_d(V)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:genericRigidityMatroid-indep-iff}
  Let $q$ be a placement of $S$ in $\R^d$, and let $p$ be a placement of
  $V$ that agrees with $q$ on $S$. The row at $p$ of an edge of $H$ is
  its row at $q$ composed with the restriction
  $\rho \colon (\R^d)^V \to (\R^d)^S$, as in the proof of
  \cref{lem:isSparse-of-rowIndependent-two}. Since $\rho$ is surjective,
  the rows of the edges of $H$ at $p$ are linearly independent if and
  only if their rows at $q$ are. Every placement of $S$ extends to one
  of $V$, and every placement of $V$ restricts to one of $S$. By
  \cref{lem:genericRigidityMatroid-indep-iff}, this proves the first
  claim.

  The inclusion maps the subsets of $E(H)$ bijectively onto the subsets
  of its image, preserving their size. By the first claim, applied to
  the subgraphs of $H$, it also preserves independence. The rank of a
  set is the largest size of an independent subset, so the two ranks
  are equal.
\end{proof}

The reduction also uses four facts about squares and induced subgraphs.

\begin{lemma}[Deleting the edges at a vertex of degree at most one
  commutes with squaring]
  \label{lem:square-delete-degree-le-one}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.square_deleteIncidenceSet_of_degree_le_one}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph}
  Let $G$ be a graph on $V$, and let $v$ be a vertex of degree at most
  one in $G$. Then
  \[
    \bigl(G - E_G(v)\bigr)^2 \;=\; G^2 - E_{G^2}(v).
  \]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Neither side has an edge at $v$. Let $x \ne y$ be vertices other than
  $v$. If $v$ were a common neighbor of $x$ and $y$, it would have two
  neighbors. So $x$ and $y$ are adjacent or have a common neighbor in
  $G$ if and only if they do in $G - E_G(v)$. That is, $xy$ is an edge
  of $G^2$ if and only if it is an edge of $(G - E_G(v))^2$
  (\cref{def:square-graph}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The square-neighborhood of a degree-one vertex]
  \label{lem:square-leaf-neighborhood}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.neighborSet_square_of_degree_eq_one,
    SimpleGraph.ncard_neighborSet_square_of_degree_eq_one,
    SimpleGraph.isClique_neighborSet_square_of_degree_eq_one}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph}
  Let $G$ be a graph on $V$, and let $v$ be a vertex of degree one in
  $G$, with neighbor $u$. Then
  \[
    N_{G^2}(v) \;=\; N_G[u] \setminus \{v\},
  \]
  where $N_G[u] = N_G(u) \cup \{u\}$ is the closed neighborhood of $u$.
  Moreover, $d_{G^2}(v) = d_G(u)$, and $N_{G^2}(v)$ is a clique of
  $G^2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{lem:square-cliques}
  Let $w \ne v$. If $w$ is adjacent to $v$, then $w = u$. If $w$ and $v$
  have a common neighbor, then it is $u$, so $w \in N_G(u)$. Conversely,
  $u$ is adjacent to $v$, and every vertex of $N_G(u)$ other than $v$
  has the common neighbor $u$ with $v$. This proves the identity. As
  $v \in N_G(u)$, the set $N_G[u] \setminus \{v\}$ has $d_G(u)$
  elements. It lies in $N_G[u]$, which is a clique of $G^2$
  (\cref{lem:square-cliques}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Squaring commutes with restriction to the support]
  \label{lem:square-induce-support}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.square_induce_of_support_subset, SimpleGraph.square_support_subset}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:square-graph}
  Let $G$ be a graph on $V$, and let $S \subseteq V$ contain every
  non-isolated vertex of $G$. Then the square of the induced subgraph
  $G[S]$ is the subgraph of $G^2$ induced on $S$, and every edge of
  $G^2$ has both ends in $S$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  Each end of an edge of $G^2$ lies on an edge of $G$, so it lies in
  $S$. Let $x \ne y$ lie in $S$. An edge $xy$ of $G$ is an edge of
  $G[S]$. A common neighbor of $x$ and $y$ in $G$ lies on an edge of
  $G$, so it lies in $S$, and it is a common neighbor of $x$ and $y$ in
  $G[S]$. So $xy$ is an edge of $G[S]^2$ if and only if it is an edge of
  $G^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The Laman condition restricts to induced subgraphs]
  \label{lem:isLaman3-induce}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.IsLaman3.induce}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:isLaman3}
  Let $G$ be a Laman graph on $V$, and let $S \subseteq V$. Then the
  induced subgraph $G[S]$ is Laman.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  A set $X \subseteq S$ of at least three vertices spans the same edges
  in $G[S]$ as in $G$, and so at most $3|X| - 6$ of them.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Jacobs' conjecture}
\label{sec:jacobs-theorem}

In the proof of their Theorem~5.4, JJ treat graphs of minimum degree
at least two, and reduce the general case to them in a single sentence:
if $G$ has maximum degree at most three, then $G^2$ is independent if
and only if the square of its maximal subgraph of minimum degree at
least two is. We prove this reduction by induction on the number of
edges, for the graphs whose square is Laman (\cref{thm:jacobs}). These
graphs have maximum degree at most three
(\cref{lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three}).

\begin{theorem}[Jacobs' conjecture, minimum degree two]
  \label{thm:jacobs-min-degree-two}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.jacobs_min_degree_two}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:isLaman3, def:square-graph, def:genericRigidityMatroid}
  Let $G$ be a simple graph of minimum degree at least two on a finite
  nonempty vertex set $V$. If $G^2$ is Laman, then $E(G^2)$ is
  independent in $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{thm:laman-square-count, cor:molecule-rank-formula}
  The rank of a finite set is at most its size, with equality if and
  only if the set is independent. By \cref{thm:laman-square-count} and
  the rank formula (\cref{cor:molecule-rank-formula}),
  \[
    |E(G^2)| \;\le\; 3|V| - 6 - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G)
    \;=\; r(G^2) \;\le\; |E(G^2)|.
  \]
  So $r(G^2) = |E(G^2)|$, and $E(G^2)$ is independent.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Jacobs' conjecture; JJ Conjecture 5.1, Theorem 5.4]
  \label{thm:jacobs}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.jacobs}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:isLaman3, def:square-graph, def:genericRigidityMatroid}
  Let $G$ be a simple graph on a finite vertex set $V$. Then $E(G^2)$
  is independent in $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$ if and only if $G^2$ is Laman.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{cor:genericMatroid-indep-isLaman3, thm:jacobs-min-degree-two,
    lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three, lem:isLaman3-mono,
    cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three, lem:genericMatroid-induce-transport,
    lem:square-delete-degree-le-one, lem:square-leaf-neighborhood,
    lem:square-induce-support, lem:isLaman3-induce}
  Independence implies the Laman condition by
  \cref{cor:genericMatroid-indep-isLaman3}. For the converse, suppose
  that $G^2$ is Laman. We show that $E(G^2)$ is independent, by
  induction on the number of edges of $G$.

  Suppose first that $G$ has a vertex $v$ of degree one, with neighbor
  $u$, and let $G' = G - E_G(v)$. Then $G'^2 = G^2 - E_{G^2}(v)$
  (\cref{lem:square-delete-degree-le-one}). So $G'^2$ is Laman
  (\cref{lem:isLaman3-mono}), and $E(G'^2)$ is independent by
  induction, as $G'$ has one edge fewer than $G$. In $G^2$ the vertex
  $v$ has degree $d_G(u)$ (\cref{lem:square-leaf-neighborhood}), and
  $d_G(u) \le 3$ (\cref{lem:isLaman3-degree-le-three}). So $E(G^2)$ is
  independent because $E(G'^2)$ is
  (\cref{cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three}).

  Otherwise every vertex of $G$ has degree zero or at least two. If $G$
  has no edges, then $G^2$ has none either, and the empty set is
  independent. Else let $S$ be the set of non-isolated vertices of $G$.
  Every edge of $G^2$ lies inside $S$, and the square of the induced
  subgraph $G[S]$ is the subgraph of $G^2$ induced on $S$
  (\cref{lem:square-induce-support}). So $G[S]^2$ is Laman
  (\cref{lem:isLaman3-induce}). A vertex of $S$ has the same neighbors
  in $G[S]$ as in $G$, so $G[S]$ has minimum degree at least two. Hence
  $E(G[S]^2)$ is independent in $\mathcal{R}_3(S)$
  (\cref{thm:jacobs-min-degree-two}, on the nonempty set $S$). Its image
  under the inclusion $S \hookrightarrow V$ is $E(G^2)$, which is
  therefore independent in $\mathcal{R}_3(V)$
  (\cref{lem:genericMatroid-induce-transport}).
\end{proof}

\subsection{The degree-one rank formula}
\label{sec:jacobs-degree-one}

The rank formula (\cref{cor:molecule-rank-formula}) assumes minimum
degree at least two, and it fails without this assumption. For a single
edge, $r(G^2) = 1$, while $3|V| - 6 - \operatorname{def}(\tilde G) = -1$,
as the partition of the two vertices into singletons has value one.
JJ's Lemma~4.2 gives $r(G^2)$ for every connected graph $G$ on at least
two vertices. Its part~(a) gives it explicitly for trees. Its part~(b)
gives it, for the other graphs, through the rank of the square of the
core of $G$, its maximal subgraph of minimum degree at least two.
JJ prove both parts by deleting a vertex of degree one, and cite their
Lemma~3.3 for the change in rank. Here that step is
\cref{cor:zero-extension-clique-rank}, as the deleted vertex may have
more than three neighbors in $G^2$.

We write $V_1(G)$ for the set of vertices of degree one of a graph $G$,
and call the set of its non-isolated vertices its \emph{support}. A
graph on a subset $S$ of $V$ is also a graph on $V$, in which the
vertices outside $S$ are isolated, and its rank is the same in both
readings (\cref{lem:genericMatroid-induce-transport}). So for a vertex
$v$ of $G$ we write $G - v$ for the graph obtained by deleting $v$, and
read its rank either way. In the definition of the core, the minimum
degree of a subgraph is taken over its support.

\begin{definition}[The core]
  \label{def:two-core}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.twoCore, SimpleGraph.twoCore_le, SimpleGraph.le_twoCore,
    SimpleGraph.twoCore_minDegree}
  \leanok
  Let $G$ be a graph on $V$. The \emph{core} $G^{\mathrm{core}}$ of $G$
  is its maximal subgraph of minimum degree at least two: the graph on
  $V$ whose edges are those of all subgraphs $H$ of $G$ in which every
  vertex of the support of $H$ has degree at least two in $H$.
\end{definition}

The core is itself one of these subgraphs $H$. Indeed, a vertex of its
support lies on an edge of some such $H$. So it has at least two
neighbors in $H$, and hence in the core. Thus the core is the largest
subgraph of $G$ in which every vertex of the support has degree at
least two.

As JJ note, the core of a connected graph on at least two vertices has
no edges if and only if the graph is a tree. Indeed, a subgraph of a
tree that has an edge is a forest with an edge, so it has a vertex of
degree one. A connected graph that is not a tree contains a cycle, in
which every vertex has degree two. For a tree, the core has empty
support, and the right-hand side of the formula of
\cref{thm:degree-one-rank} would be $2|V| + |V_1(G)|$. This is five more
than $r(G^2)$ (\cref{thm:degree-one-rank-tree}), so trees are treated
separately.

\begin{theorem}[The degree-one rank formula for trees; JJ Lemma 4.2(a)]
  \label{thm:degree-one-rank-tree}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.degree_one_rank_tree}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:genericRank, def:square-graph}
  Let $G$ be a tree on a finite vertex set $V$ with $|V| \ge 2$. Then
  \[
    r(G^2) \;=\; 2|V| - 5 + |V_1(G)| .
  \]
  JJ credit the formula to Franzblau~\cite{franzblau2000}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{cor:zero-extension-clique-rank, cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three,
    lem:square-delete-degree-le-one, lem:square-leaf-neighborhood}
  We induct on $|V|$. If $|V| = 2$, then $G$ is a single edge, and
  $G^2 = G$. Deleting the edge at one of its ends leaves no edge, so
  $r(G^2) = 1$ (\cref{cor:zero-extension-degree-le-three}). And
  $2 \cdot 2 - 5 + 2 = 1$.

  Let $|V| \ge 3$, and let $v$ be a vertex of degree one, with neighbor
  $u$. Then $d_G(u) \ge 2$, as otherwise $\{u, v\}$ would be a connected
  component of $G$. The graph $G - v$ is a tree on $|V| - 1$ vertices,
  and its square is $G^2 - v$ (\cref{lem:square-delete-degree-le-one}).
  In $G^2$ the vertex $v$ has $d_G(u)$ neighbors, and they form a clique
  (\cref{lem:square-leaf-neighborhood}). So
  \[
    r(G^2) \;=\; r\bigl((G - v)^2\bigr) + \min\bigl(3, d_G(u)\bigr)
  \]
  (\cref{cor:zero-extension-clique-rank}).

  The term $2|V|$ is two less for $G - v$ than for $G$. If
  $d_G(u) \ge 3$, then $V_1(G - v) = V_1(G) \setminus \{v\}$, so the
  right-hand side of the formula is three less for $G - v$. If
  $d_G(u) = 2$, then $V_1(G - v)$ is $V_1(G)$ with $u$ in place of $v$,
  so the right-hand side is two less. In both cases it falls by
  $\min(3, d_G(u))$, as the rank does. So the formula for $G - v$, which
  holds by induction, gives the formula for $G$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The degree-one rank formula; JJ Lemma 4.2(b)]
  \label{thm:degree-one-rank}
  \lean{SimpleGraph.degree_one_rank}
  \leanok
  \uses{def:two-core, def:genericRank, def:square-graph}
  Let $G$ be a connected simple graph on a finite vertex set $V$ with
  $|V| \ge 2$ that is not a tree. Then
  \[
    r(G^2) \;=\; r\bigl((G^{\mathrm{core}})^2\bigr)
      + 2\,\bigl|V \setminus V(G^{\mathrm{core}})\bigr|
      + |V_1(G)| ,
  \]
  where $V(G^{\mathrm{core}})$ is the support of the core.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  \leanok
  \uses{cor:zero-extension-clique-rank, lem:square-delete-degree-le-one,
    lem:square-leaf-neighborhood}
  We induct on $|V|$. If $V_1(G)$ is empty, then every vertex of $G$
  has degree at least two, as $G$ is connected and $|V| \ge 2$. So $G$
  is its own core, its support is $V$, and both sides equal $r(G^2)$.

  Otherwise let $v$ be a vertex of degree one, with neighbor $u$. Then
  $d_G(u) \ge 2$, as otherwise $G$ would be the single edge $uv$, a
  tree. A connected graph is a tree exactly when it has one edge fewer
  than vertices, so $G$ has at least as many edges as vertices. Deleting
  $v$ removes one vertex and one edge. So $G - v$ is connected, has at
  least two vertices, and is not a tree. As in the proof of
  \cref{thm:degree-one-rank-tree},
  $r(G^2) = r((G - v)^2) + \min(3, d_G(u))$.

  Deleting $v$ does not change the core. Indeed, a subgraph of $G$ in
  which every vertex of the support has degree at least two has no edge
  at $v$, as $v$ has degree one. So $G$ and $G - v$ have the same such
  subgraphs. In particular $v$ is not in $V(G^{\mathrm{core}})$, and
  $|V \setminus V(G^{\mathrm{core}})|$ is one less for $G - v$ than for
  $G$. The set $V_1$ changes as in the proof of
  \cref{thm:degree-one-rank-tree}. So the right-hand side falls by
  $2 + 1 = 3$ if $d_G(u) \ge 3$, and by $2$ if $d_G(u) = 2$. In both
  cases it falls by $\min(3, d_G(u))$, as the rank does, and the formula
  for $G - v$, which holds by induction, gives the formula for $G$.
\end{proof}

\begin{fmlnote}
  \label{fmlnote:degree-one-fixed-carrier}
  The two inductions are formalized on the number of edges, over a fixed
  vertex set. Deleting the edge at $v$ leaves $v$ in $V$ as an isolated
  vertex, so the formal proofs do not change the vertex set of the
  matroid, and they do not use \cref{lem:genericMatroid-induce-transport}.
  They prove stronger statements, in which $|V|$ is the size of the
  support of $G$, connectivity is asked only between vertices of the
  support, and, for \cref{thm:degree-one-rank}, ``not a tree'' is the
  bound $|E(G)| \ge |V(G)|$, with $V(G)$ the support of $G$. The core is
  a graph on $V$, and $V(G^{\mathrm{core}})$ is its support. The formal
  statement of \cref{thm:degree-one-rank} does not assume $|V| \ge 2$,
  which follows from its other hypotheses, as a connected graph on one
  vertex is a tree. The tree formula is formalized as
  $r(G^2) + 5 = 2|V| + |V_1(G)|$, which avoids a subtraction of natural
  numbers.
\end{fmlnote}
